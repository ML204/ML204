<Section label="What is it?">

The **learning rate** (written $\alpha$) is a small positive number that controls how big each step is during gradient descent. It is one of the most important settings in machine learning.

</Section>

<Section label="Why we need it">

Gradient descent knows which direction to move (downhill), but not how far to step.

If you take tiny steps, you will eventually reach the minimum — but it will take a very long time.

If you take huge steps, you might jump over the minimum and end up somewhere worse. Take even bigger steps and the loss can grow instead of shrink — the model **diverges**.

The learning rate is the dial that controls step size. Finding a good value is one of the first things you tune in any ML project.

</Section>

<Divider />

<Section label="Explained in detail">

Recall the gradient descent update rule:

$$
w \leftarrow w - \alpha \cdot \nabla L(w)
$$

The gradient $\nabla L$ tells us the direction and steepness of the slope. The learning rate $\alpha$ scales that by telling us: *of that full slope, what fraction of a step do we take?*

**Too small ($\alpha \approx 0.001$):** Each step barely moves. The ball creeps toward the minimum. Training takes thousands of steps. It may never get there in a reasonable time.

**Just right ($\alpha \approx 0.1$):** Steps are large enough to converge in tens to hundreds of steps, but small enough not to overshoot. The loss decreases smoothly.

**Too large ($\alpha \approx 1.0+$):** Each step overshoots the minimum. The ball lands on the other side of the valley, then jumps back over again — going further each time. The loss oscillates or grows. The model **diverges**.

<Analogy>
  Imagine adjusting the temperature on a shower. Tiny turns and you wait forever for it to warm up. Huge turns and you slam between freezing and scalding. A moderate turn gets you to the right temperature quickly and smoothly.
</Analogy>

**How do you pick a learning rate in practice?**

1. Start around $0.01$ or $0.1$ and watch the loss curve.
2. If the loss decreases smoothly → good.
3. If the loss barely moves → try 10× larger.
4. If the loss oscillates or increases → try 10× smaller.
5. Use a **learning rate schedule** to start larger and decrease over time.

</Section>

<Callout kind="tip">
  A good rule of thumb: plot the loss vs. steps for a few values of α (e.g. 0.001, 0.01, 0.1). The best α is the one where the loss falls steeply and smoothly. This plot is called the **loss curve**.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="info">
  **Learning rate schedules** start with a larger α early in training (when the model is far from the minimum and big steps are fine) and reduce it later (when you need fine adjustments near the minimum). Common schedules: step decay, cosine annealing, warmup.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning">
  **Common mistake:** choosing a learning rate once and never changing it. In practice, the best α for a large model at step 1 is not the same as at step 10 000.
</Callout>
