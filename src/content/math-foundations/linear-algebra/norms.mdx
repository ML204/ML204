<Section label="What is it?">

A **vector norm** is a mathematical ruler that assigns a single non-negative number to any vector to measure its **size (length)** or the **distance** between two data points.

Because there is more than one way to walk from corner A to corner B—along city streets versus flying straight across rooftops—machine learning uses several different norms, especially the **$L_1$ (Manhattan)** norm and the **$L_2$ (Euclidean)** norm.

</Section>

<Section label="Why we need it">

Choosing *how* you measure distance or parameter size completely changes how a machine learning model behaves:

- **Measuring Prediction Error (Loss Functions)**: Mean Absolute Error (MAE) relies on the $L_1$ norm and resists extreme outliers, while Mean Squared Error (MSE) uses the squared $L_2$ norm and penalizes huge mistakes heavily.
- **Regularization & Model Simplicity**: Adding an $L_1$ penalty (**Lasso**) drives useless feature weights all the way to **exact zero** (automatic feature selection), while an $L_2$ penalty (**Ridge**) shrinks weights smoothly toward zero without discarding features.
- **Nearest Neighbors & Search ($k$-NN, Clustering)**: K-Means and $k$-Nearest Neighbors depend directly on distance metrics ($\|\mathbf{x}_a - \mathbf{x}_b\|_2$ or $\|\mathbf{x}_a - \mathbf{x}_b\|_1$) to decide which customers or images look alike.
- **Gradient Clipping in Deep Learning**: Prevents exploding gradients in neural networks by rescaling weight updates whenever $\|\nabla \mathcal{L}\|_2$ grows beyond a safe threshold.

</Section>

<Section label="Explained in detail">

### 1. The City Grid vs The Helicopter: $L_1$ and $L_2$ Intuition

Suppose you stand at the origin $(0, 0)$ and need to travel to $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$—three blocks East and four blocks North.

![Comparing L1 city block walk against L2 straight helicopter line for vector [3, 4]](/images/norms/01_city_blocks_vs_crow.png)

1. **$L_1$ Norm (Manhattan / Taxicab Distance)**: You must stick to perpendicular streets. Total blocks driven = sum of absolute steps:
   $$
   \|\mathbf{v}\|_1 = |3| + |4| = 7
   $$
2. **$L_2$ Norm (Euclidean Distance)**: You fly diagonally "as the crow flies" using the Pythagorean theorem:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \|\mathbf{v}\|_2 &= \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} \\
   &= \sqrt{25} = 5
   \end{aligned}
   $$
3. **$L_\infty$ Norm (Max / Chebyshev Distance)**: Only the single largest coordinate matters:
   $$
   \|\mathbf{v}\|_\infty = \max(|3|, |4|) = 4
   $$

<Analogy>
  If a warehouse robot can only roll along North–South and East–West aisles, travel time equals the **$L_1$ distance**. If an aerial drone flies straight over the shelves, travel distance equals the **$L_2$ distance**. If two motors run simultaneously—one moving $x$ and one moving $y$—the total time until arrival equals the slower axis: the **$L_\infty$ distance**!
</Analogy>

Notice an important inequality that *always* holds for every vector $\mathbf{v}$:

$$
\|\mathbf{v}\|_\infty \le \|\mathbf{v}\|_2 \le \|\mathbf{v}\|_1
$$

In our worked example: $4 \le 5 \le 7$. The three norms only become equal when all coordinates except one are zero (e.g. $[5, 0]^T$).

---

### 2. The General $L_p$ Norm & Distance Between Points

All of these belong to one unified family called **$L_p$ norms** (for $p \ge 1$):

$$
\|\mathbf{x}\|_p = \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i|^p \right)^{1/p}
$$

- Set $p = 1 \implies \|\mathbf{x}\|_1 = \sum |x_i|$
- Set $p = 2 \implies \|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum x_i^2}$
- Let $p \to \infty \implies \|\mathbf{x}\|_\infty = \max_i |x_i|$

#### Turning a Norm Into a Distance Metric
To measure distance between two vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, simply subtract them first and take the norm of the difference:

$$
\text{dist}_p(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\|_p
$$

Worked example with $\mathbf{a} = [1, 5]^T$ and $\mathbf{b} = [4, 1]^T$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 - 4 \\ 5 - 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \\
\|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\|_1 &= |-3| + |4| = 7 \\
\|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\|_2 &= \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 4^2} = 5
\end{aligned}
$$

---

### 3. Unit Balls: Why Shape Matters So Much in ML

What does the set of all points with norm $\le 1$ look like? Those shapes—called **unit balls**—explain one of the most famous results in all of Machine Learning!

![L1 diamond, L2 circle, and L-infinity square unit balls compared side by side](/images/norms/02_unit_balls_comparison.png)

- **$L_1$ Unit Ball**: A **diamond** with sharp corners poking out along the coordinate axes!
- **$L_2$ Unit Ball**: A perfectly smooth **circle** (sphere in higher dimensions) with zero corners.
- **$L_\infty$ Unit Ball**: A **square** aligned with the coordinate grid.

#### Why $L_1$ Creates Sparse Models (Lasso) While $L_2$ Keeps Everything Smooth (Ridge)

Suppose your training loss contours (dashed ellipses) expand outward until they first touch your budget constraint shape:

![Geometric comparison showing L1 diamond corner intersecting axis at zero weight while L2 circle touches smoothly off-axis](/images/norms/03_l1_sparsity_vs_l2_ridge.png)

Because the $L_1$ diamond has **pointy corners sitting directly on the axes** ($w_2 = 0$ or $w_1 = 0$), an expanding loss ellipse almost always collides with a corner first—snapping unimportant feature weights cleanly to **exact zero**! Meanwhile, a smooth $L_2$ circle touches the ellipse along its curved rim, shrinking both weights gently while keeping both non-zero.

---

### 4. What Makes a Function a Valid Norm? (3 Rules)

Mathematicians call $\|\cdot\|$ a valid norm only if it satisfies three sanity checks:
1. **Non-negativity**: $\|\mathbf{v}\| \ge 0$, and $\|\mathbf{v}\| = 0$ *only* when $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.
2. **Homogeneity (Scaling)**: $\|c\mathbf{v}\| = |c| \, \|\mathbf{v}\|$ (doubling a vector doubles its length).
3. **Triangle Inequality**: $\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\| \le \|\mathbf{u}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|$ (taking a detour through two arrows can never be shorter than going straight).

<Callout kind="tip">
  **Normalization ($\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{v} / \|\mathbf{v}\|_2$)**: Dividing a non-zero vector by its own $L_2$ norm creates a **unit vector** pointing in the exact same direction with length equal to $1$. ML pipelines do this constantly before computing cosine similarities!
</Callout>

<Section label="Notes & Formula">

<Formula label="L1, L2, and Max Norms">
  $\|\mathbf{x}\|_1 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i|$
  <br />
  $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2}$
  <br />
  $\|\mathbf{x}\|_\infty = \max_{1 \le i \le n} |x_i|$
</Formula>

<Formula label="General Lp Norm & Triangle Inequality">
  $\|\mathbf{x}\|_p = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i|^p\right)^{1/p}$
  <br />
  $\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\|_p \le \|\mathbf{u}\|_p + \|\mathbf{v}\|_p$
</Formula>

<Callout kind="warning">
  **Common Confusion: $L_0$ "Norm"**: You will often hear ML papers talk about the "$L_0$ norm" as a count of non-zero entries. Technically it is *not* a true mathematical norm because scaling $[1, 0]$ by $5$ keeps $1$ non-zero entry (violating homogeneity $\|c\mathbf{v}\| = |c|\|\mathbf{v}\|$). That's why we use the **$L_1$ norm** as the closest convex proxy for counting non-zero weights!
</Callout>
