<Section label="What is it?">

When a square matrix $\mathbf{A}$ transforms space—stretching, tilting, or reflecting the grid—nearly every vector gets knocked off its original straight line.

However, a few special non-zero directions stay **strictly on their own line**! When $\mathbf{A}$ hits one of these special vectors, it *never* twists sideways—it only stretches, shrinks, or flips the arrow in place. Those special invariant vectors are **eigenvectors** ($\mathbf{v}$), and their scaling factor is the matching **eigenvalue** ($\lambda$):

$$
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}
$$

</Section>

<Section label="Why we need it">

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors uncover the hidden "natural skeleton" of a matrix or dataset:

- **Principal Component Analysis (PCA)**: The eigenvectors of a dataset's covariance matrix reveal the perpendicular axes where data varies the most, letting us compress $1000$ correlated features into a handful of informative coordinates!
- **Google PageRank**: Ranking billions of web pages finds the dominant eigenvector of the web link matrix.
- **Matrix Powers & Dynamical Systems**: Predicting what happens after $100$ time steps ($\mathbf{A}^{100}\mathbf{x}$) reduces from multiplying matrices $100$ times to simply raising scalar eigenvalues to $\lambda^{100}$!
- **Hessians & Optimization Curvature**: Eigenvalues of the loss surface's Hessian matrix tell optimizers whether you sit inside a smooth bowl (all $\lambda > 0$), on a flat plateau ($\lambda \approx 0$), or at a tricky saddle point (mixed signs).

</Section>

<Section label="Explained in detail">

### 1. Knocked Off Course vs Staying on Track

Imagine spinning a globe on its stand. Every city on the equator whips around in circles—its direction constantly changes. Only **the North–South axis** passes straight through both poles and stays aligned in place! That spin axis is literally the globe rotation's real eigenvector (with eigenvalue $\lambda = 1$).

![Comparison showing ordinary vector v knocked off its line versus eigenvector e1 staying on its span scaled by lambda](/images/eigenvalues-eigenvectors/01_stretch_vs_knocked_off_span.png)

<Analogy>
  Think of eigenvectors as the **grain of a piece of wood** or the **stretch seams of a sheet of fabric**: pull diagonally across the sheet, and most drawn arrows twist crooked, while arrows drawn straight along the main stretch directions simply lengthen or shorten along the exact same ruler line!
</Analogy>

---

### 2. What Every Value of $\lambda$ Means Geometrically

Once you find an eigenvector $\mathbf{v}$, the scalar eigenvalue $\lambda$ tells you everything that happens along that line:

![Four geometric behaviors of lambda: stretch > 1, shrink between 0 and 1, negative flip, and zero collapse](/images/eigenvalues-eigenvectors/02_eigenvalue_behaviors.png)

| Eigenvalue $\lambda$ | Geometric Effect Along $\mathbf{v}$ | Example Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$\lambda > 1$** | Arrow stretches longer in the same direction | High-variance data axis in PCA |
| **$0 < \lambda < 1$** | Arrow shrinks shorter in the same direction | Low-variance / noise direction |
| **$\lambda < 0$** | Arrow flips $180^\circ$ backwards and scales by $|\lambda|$ | Mirror reflection axis |
| **$\lambda = 0$** | Entire direction collapses to $\mathbf{0}$ | Matrix is **singular** ($\det(\mathbf{A}) = 0$) |
| **Complex ($a \pm bi$)** | No real line stays fixed; space swirls/rotates! | Pure $90^\circ$ rotation matrix |

---

### 3. Finding Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors Step by Step (2×2 Worked Example)

How do we solve $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}$ for a concrete matrix $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$?

Rewrite the equation with the identity matrix $\mathbf{I}$ so both sides subtract vectors cleanly:

$$
(\mathbf{A} - \lambda \mathbf{I})\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}
$$

For a **non-zero** vector $\mathbf{v}$ to get crushed to $\mathbf{0}$, the matrix $(\mathbf{A} - \lambda \mathbf{I})$ must squash space flat—which means its determinant MUST equal zero:

$$
\det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda \mathbf{I}) = 0
$$

#### Step 1: Solve the Characteristic Equation for $\lambda$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\det\begin{pmatrix} 2 - \lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2 - \lambda \end{pmatrix} &= 0 \\
(2 - \lambda)^2 - 1 &= 0 \\
\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 &= 0 \\
(\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1) &= 0 \\
\implies \lambda_1 = 3, &\quad \lambda_2 = 1
\end{aligned}
$$

*(Shortcut check for $2 \times 2$ matrices: $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = 4 = \text{tr}(\mathbf{A})$, and $\lambda_1 \cdot \lambda_2 = 3 = \det(\mathbf{A})$.)*

#### Step 2: Plug Each $\lambda$ Back In to Find Its Eigenvector
- **For $\lambda_1 = 3$**:
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  (\mathbf{A} - 3\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v} &= \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} \\
  &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies v_1 = v_2
  \end{aligned}
  $$
  Any multiple of $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ works! Check:
  $$
  \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
  $$

- **For $\lambda_2 = 1$**:
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  (\mathbf{A} - 1\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} \\
  &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies v_1 = -v_2
  \end{aligned}
  $$
  Any multiple of $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$ works! Notice $[1, 1]^T \cdot [1, -1]^T = 0$—they are **perpendicular**!

---

### 4. Symmetric Matrices & PCA: Machine Learning's Best Friend

Notice $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ was **symmetric** ($\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}^T$). Symmetric matrices satisfy the **Spectral Theorem**, giving two magical guarantees:
1. All eigenvalues are **guaranteed to be real numbers** (no imaginary swirls!).
2. Eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are **guaranteed to be orthogonal ($90^\circ$ perpendicular)**.

Because a dataset's covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is *always* symmetric, **Principal Component Analysis (PCA)** finds perpendicular axes ranked from largest eigenvalue ($\lambda_1$ = direction of widest data spread) to smallest ($\lambda_2$ = narrowest noise axis):

![PCA eigenvectors aligned cleanly along the major and minor variance axes of a tilted 2D data cloud](/images/eigenvalues-eigenvectors/03_pca_eigen_intuition.png)

<Section label="Notes & Formula">

<Formula label="Eigenvalue Equation">
  $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v} \quad (\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0})$
  <br />
  $\det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda \mathbf{I}) = 0$
</Formula>

<Formula label="Trace, Determinant & Spectral Form">
  $\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i = \text{tr}(\mathbf{A})$
  <br />
  $\prod_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i = \det(\mathbf{A})$
  <br />
  $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{Q}^T \quad (\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}^T)$
</Formula>

<Callout kind="warning">
  **Zero Vector Exception**: Notice $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{0} = \lambda \mathbf{0}$ is trivially true for *every* number $\lambda$, which is why the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ is **never allowed to be an eigenvector**—it carries zero directional information! However, the **eigenvalue $\lambda = 0$** *is* completely valid (and signals a singular matrix!).
</Callout>
