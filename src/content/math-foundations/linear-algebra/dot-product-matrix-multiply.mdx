<Section label="What is it?">

The **dot product** takes two vectors of the same length and reduces them to **one single number** measuring how strongly they point in the same direction.

**Matrix multiplication** extends that idea to entire grids of numbers: each entry in the resulting matrix is just a dot product between one row on the left and one column on the right. Instead of transforming one number at a time, a matrix transforms an entire coordinate space—rotating, stretching, or shearing every vector simultaneously.

</Section>

<Section label="Why we need it">

Nearly every computation inside modern AI systems boils down to chained dot products and matrix multiplications:

- **Search & Semantic Similarity**: Search engines and vector databases score how closely a user query matches millions of documents using **cosine similarity**—a normalized dot product between embedding vectors.
- **Neural Network Layers**: Every dense layer and Transformer attention head computes weighted sums of inputs via $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$, which is literally one dot product per output neuron executed in parallel on GPUs.
- **Attention Mechanisms**: Large Language Models decide which words should attend to which earlier words by taking dot products between **Query** and **Key** vectors ($\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^T$).
- **Linear Regression Predictions**: Computing predicted house prices for $10\,000$ homes at once is a single matrix-vector multiply $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}$.

</Section>

### 1. The Dot Product: Two Views That Mean the Exact Same Thing

Suppose you have two 2D vectors $\mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

#### View A: Pairwise Multiply & Add (Algebraic)
Multiply matching slots together and sum the products:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} &= a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 \\
&= (3)(4) + (2)(1) \\
&= 12 + 2 = 14
\end{aligned}
$$

<Analogy>
  Think of a grocery shopping receipt: $\mathbf{a} = [3, 2]^T$ is the quantity of items you bought (3 apples, 2 coffees) and $\mathbf{b} = [4, 1]^T$ is the price of each item (\$4, \$1). Their dot product $3 \times 4 + 2 \times 1 = 14$ gives your total bill of **\$14**!
</Analogy>

#### View B: Alignment & Shadow (Geometric)
Geometrically, the dot product measures how much one vector helps push along another:

$$
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\| \, \|\mathbf{b}\| \cos(\theta)
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the two arrows.

![Three dot product cases: positive alignment, orthogonal zero, and negative opposite](/images/dot-product-matrix-multiply/01_dot_product_angles.png)

Look at what the sign of $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$ immediately tells you:
- **Positive ($\theta < 90^\circ$)**: Vectors point roughly the same way.
- **Zero ($\theta = 90^\circ$)**: Vectors are **orthogonal** (perpendicular)—neither has any shadow along the other!
- **Negative ($\theta > 90^\circ$)**: Vectors point in opposing directions.

#### Orthogonal Projection (Shining a Flashlight)
Imagine shining a flashlight perpendicular onto vector $\mathbf{b}$. The shadow that $\mathbf{a}$ casts onto the line of $\mathbf{b}$ is its **orthogonal projection**:

$$
\text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a}) = \left( \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{b}\|^2} \right) \mathbf{b}
$$

Notice that $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$ equals **(signed length of $\mathbf{a}$'s shadow along $\mathbf{b}$) $\times$ $\|\mathbf{b}\|$**.

![Orthogonal shadow projection of vector a onto vector b](/images/dot-product-matrix-multiply/02_shadow_projection.png)

### 2. Cosine Similarity: Pure Direction Without Length Bias

Suppose Document A repeats every word $10$ times compared to Document B, even though both talk about the exact same topic (Machine Learning). Document A's vector is $10\times$ longer, so its raw dot product gets inflated $10\times$!

To compare **direction (meaning) alone**, divide by both vector lengths so length scales out completely:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{cos\_sim}(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) &= \cos(\theta) \\
&= \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\| \, \|\mathbf{b}\|} \in [-1, 1]
\end{aligned}
$$

- **$+1$**: Identical direction ($\theta = 0^\circ$).
- **$0$**: Unrelated / orthogonal ($\theta = 90^\circ$).
- **$-1$**: Opposite meaning ($\theta = 180^\circ$).

### 3. Matrix-Vector Multiplication ($\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$): How Matrices Warp Space

What does multiplying a $2 \times 2$ matrix $\mathbf{A}$ by a vector $\mathbf{x}$ actually *do*? There are two equally important views:

1. **Row View (Stacked Dot Products)**: Each output coordinate is the dot product between one row of $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{x}$.
2. **Column View (Moving the Grid Basis)**: Every 2D vector $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}$ is built from $x_1$ steps East ($\hat{\mathbf{i}} = [1,0]^T$) plus $x_2$ steps North ($\hat{\mathbf{j}} = [0,1]^T$). When $\mathbf{A}$ multiplies $\mathbf{x}$, **$\mathbf{A}$'s columns tell you exactly where those two unit basis arrows land**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} &= \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} \\
&= x_1 \begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix} + x_2 \begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Once $\hat{\mathbf{i}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{j}}$ move to their new spots (keeping grid lines straight, parallel, and evenly spaced), **every point $\mathbf{x}$ simply rides along with the warped grid**!

![Before and after grid transformation showing basis arrows i-hat and j-hat landing on the columns of matrix A](/images/dot-product-matrix-multiply/05_basis_transformation.png)

#### Four Classic $2 \times 2$ Matrix Transformations at a Glance

| Matrix $\mathbf{A}$ | Where $\hat{\mathbf{i}}$ & $\hat{\mathbf{j}}$ land | Geometric transformation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ | $\hat{\mathbf{i}} \to [2, 0]^T$<br />$\hat{\mathbf{j}} \to [0, 1]^T$ | **Stretch** space $2\times$ wider horizontally |
| $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ | $\hat{\mathbf{i}} \to [0, 1]^T$<br />$\hat{\mathbf{j}} \to [-1, 0]^T$ | **Rotate** the whole plane $90^\circ$ counterclockwise |
| $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ | $\hat{\mathbf{i}} \to [1, 0]^T$<br />$\hat{\mathbf{j}} \to [1, 1]^T$ | **Shear** sideways (tilts squares into parallelograms) |
| $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ | $\hat{\mathbf{i}} \to [1, 0]^T$<br />$\hat{\mathbf{j}} \to [0, 0]^T$ | **Flatten** all of 2D space onto the $x_1$-axis |

Worked numerical example with $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \\
&= 4\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + 5\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix} 2(4) + 1(5) \\ 0(4) + 3(5) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 13 \\ 15 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

### 4. Matrix-Matrix Multiplication & Chaining Transformations

When you compute $(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})$, you chain **two geometric moves back-to-back, right to left**: first $\mathbf{A}$ moves $\hat{\mathbf{i}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{j}}$ into $\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{i}}$ and $\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{j}}$; next $\mathbf{B}$ takes those transformed arrows and warps them again into $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{i}})$ and $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{j}})$—which become the columns of $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$.

Algebraically, every entry $(i, j)$ of a matrix product is the dot product of **row $i$ on the left** with **column $j$ on the right**, so the inner shapes must match:

![Domino shape rule showing inner dimensions matching and row-by-column dot products](/images/dot-product-matrix-multiply/03_matrix_multiply_shapes.png)

Let's multiply a $2 \times 3$ matrix by a $3 \times 2$ matrix step by step:
- **Entry $(1,1)$**: $1(2) + 2(0) + 0(5) = 2$
- **Entry $(1,2)$**: $1(1) + 2(3) + 0(2) = 7$
- **Entry $(2,1)$**: $3(2) + 1(0) + 4(5) = 26$
- **Entry $(2,2)$**: $3(1) + 1(3) + 4(2) = 14$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \\ 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix} 2{+}0{+}0 & 1{+}6{+}0 \\ 6{+}0{+}20 & 3{+}3{+}8 \end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 7 \\ 26 & 14 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Why Order Matters: $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} \neq \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$!

With regular numbers, $3 \times 5 = 5 \times 3$. With matrices, **order is NOT interchangeable**! Think of putting on socks then shoes versus shoes then socks—or geometrically, **rotating then shearing** lands somewhere completely different from **shearing then rotating**:

![Visual proof that rotating then shearing ends at a different point than shearing then rotating](/images/dot-product-matrix-multiply/04_non_commutativity.png)

### 5. Vector Spaces, Span, Linear Independence & Linear Transformations

Understanding *where* vectors can reach unlocks the deeper structure behind Linear Algebra:

#### Linear Combinations & Span
Given vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$ and scalars $c_1, c_2$, forming $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2$ is a **linear combination**—stretching each arrow and summing them tip-to-tail. The **span** of a set of vectors is the entire collection of points you can possibly reach using every choice of $c_1, c_2$.

#### Vector Space, Linear Independence, Basis & Dimension
A **vector space** (such as $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^n$) is a playground of vectors that is *closed*: adding any two vectors or scaling by any scalar keeps you strictly inside the space (and always includes the origin $\mathbf{0}$).

- **Linear Independence**: No vector in the set is redundant (none can be built by combining the others). Two independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ point in non-parallel directions and span the **entire 2D plane**.
- **Linear Dependence**: At least one vector lies in the span of the others (e.g., $\mathbf{v}_2 = 2\mathbf{v}_1$). Their span collapses onto a trapped **1D line**.
- **Basis & Dimension**: A **basis** is a minimal set of linearly independent vectors that spans the whole space (like standard basis $\{\hat{\mathbf{i}}, \hat{\mathbf{j}}\}$). The number of vectors in the basis defines the space's **dimension** ($\dim(\mathbb{R}^2) = 2$).

![Linearly independent basis spanning 2D space vs linearly dependent vectors trapped on a 1D line](/images/dot-product-matrix-multiply/06_span_and_independence.png)

#### What Makes a Function a "Linear Transformation"?
Matrix multiplication $\mathbf{x} \mapsto \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ is the exact algebraic representation of a **linear transformation** $T$: it keeps parallel grid lines straight & evenly spaced and pins the origin at $\mathbf{0}$ by obeying two golden rules:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) &= T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v}) \\
T(c\mathbf{v}) &= c\,T(\mathbf{v})
\end{aligned}
$$

<Section label="Notes & Formula">

<Formula label="Algebraic Dot Product">
  $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{b} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i$
</Formula>

<Formula label="Geometric Dot Product">
  $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\| \, \|\mathbf{b}\| \cos(\theta)$
</Formula>

<Formula label="Shape Compatibility & Output Entry">
  $(\mathbf{A}_{m \times k})(\mathbf{B}_{k \times n}) = \mathbf{C}_{m \times n}$
  <br />
  $C_{ij} = \sum_{r=1}^{k} A_{ir} B_{rj}$
</Formula>

<Callout kind="warning">
  **Element-wise (`A * B`) vs Matrix Multiply (`A @ B`)**: In NumPy and PyTorch, `*` multiplies corresponding cells one by one (Hadamard product), whereas `@` executes true row-by-column matrix multiplication. Mixing these up is one of the most common bugs in beginner ML code!
</Callout>

<Callout kind="tip">
  Always read matrix composition **right to left**: in $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})$, transformation $\mathbf{A}$ happens to $\mathbf{x}$ *first*, and transformation $\mathbf{B}$ happens *second*.
</Callout>
