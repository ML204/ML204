<Section label="What is it?">

These three operations tell us how to **reorient**, **measure**, and **reverse** matrices:

- **Transpose ($\mathbf{A}^T$)**: Mirrors a matrix over its main diagonal, swapping its rows and columns.
- **Determinant ($\det(\mathbf{A})$)**: A single number telling you how much a square matrix stretches (or shrinks) **area/volume**, and whether it flips space inside-out like a mirror.
- **Inverse ($\mathbf{A}^{-1}$)**: The mathematical **"Undo button"** that reverses whatever transformation $\mathbf{A}$ just performed—existing **if and only if** $\det(\mathbf{A}) \neq 0$.

</Section>

<Section label="Why we need it">

Understanding transpose, determinant, and invertibility stops countless silent bugs and crashes in ML algorithms:

- **Normal Equation & Covariance Matrices**: Forming $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ pairs every pair of dataset columns using the **transpose** to measure feature correlations cleanly.
- **Multicollinearity Detection**: If two columns carry duplicate information (e.g., weight in kg and weight in lbs), $\det(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}) \approx 0$—the matrix is **singular** and OLS Regression explodes! Ridge Regression specifically adds $\lambda \mathbf{I}$ to pull the determinant safely away from zero.
- **Change-of-Variables in Generative AI (Normalizing Flows)**: Computing probability densities after warping data through neural layers requires multiplying by the Jacobian $|\det(\mathbf{J})|$ to account for volume stretching.
- **Numerical Stability**: Knowing *why* `np.linalg.solve(A, b)` beats computing explicit `np.linalg.inv(A) @ b` makes ML pipelines faster and far more accurate.

</Section>

### 1. Transpose ($\mathbf{A}^T$) & Matrix Trace ($\text{tr}(\mathbf{A})$)

#### Transpose: Swapping Rows and Columns
To compute the transpose $\mathbf{A}^T$, flip every element across the top-left-to-bottom-right diagonal so entry $(i, j)$ moves to $(j, i)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{A} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 3} \\[4pt]
\mathbf{A}^T &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}_{3 \times 2}
\end{aligned}
$$

![Transpose flips a 2x3 matrix over its diagonal into a 3x2 matrix](/images/transpose-inverse-determinant/01_transpose_mirror.png)

#### Symmetric Matrices ($\mathbf{A}^T = \mathbf{A}$)
A square matrix that remains identical after transposing is called **symmetric**—its upper-right triangle mirrors its lower-left triangle. Notice how covariance matrices $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ are always symmetric because feature $i$'s covariance with $j$ equals $j$'s covariance with $i$!

#### Matrix Trace: Sum Along the Main Diagonal
For any square $n \times n$ matrix, the **trace** ($\text{tr}(\mathbf{A})$) simply sums the numbers running down the top-left-to-bottom-right diagonal:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{A} &= \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \\
\text{tr}(\mathbf{A}) &= 5 + 3 = 8
\end{aligned}
$$

Two handy superpowers of trace in ML calculus:
- **Cyclic Shift**: $\text{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}) = \text{tr}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})$ (even when $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} \neq \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$!).
- **Eigenvalue Link**: $\text{tr}(\mathbf{A})$ always equals the sum of all eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$.

#### Essential Properties of Matrix Operations

| Property | Mathematical Rule | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Associativity** | $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}) = (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{C}$ | Grouping order does not change result |
| **Distributivity** | $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}$ | Expands like scalar algebra |
| **Non-Commutative** | $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} \neq \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$ | Swapping order changes output! |
| **Transpose Product** | $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^T = \mathbf{B}^T\mathbf{A}^T$ | Socks-and-shoes order reversal |
| **Inverse Product** | $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^{-1} = \mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ | Order reverses when undoing |

### 2. Determinant ($\det(\mathbf{A})$): How Much Does Area Stretch?

Imagine painting a $1 \times 1$ unit square (Area $= 1$) on a rubber sheet. Now stretch, shear, or rotate the entire sheet using a $2 \times 2$ matrix $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$. The old square morphs into a parallelogram whose **signed area** is the determinant:

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc
$$

![Three determinant cases: area tripled (+3), flipped orientation (-2), and squashed flat (0)](/images/transpose-inverse-determinant/02_determinant_area.png)

Read the three possibilities directly from $\det(\mathbf{A})$:
1. **$\det(\mathbf{A}) > 0$**: Areas scale by $|\det(\mathbf{A})|$ and grid orientation stays the same.
   $$
   \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix} \implies \det(\mathbf{A}) = +3.0
   $$
   (the unit square grows $3\times$ bigger).
2. **$\det(\mathbf{A}) < 0$**: Space gets **flipped upside-down** like looking in a mirror (negative signed area).
   $$
   \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0.4 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \implies \det(\mathbf{A}) = -2.0
   $$
3. **$\det(\mathbf{A}) = 0$ (Singular Matrix!)**: One dimension collapses completely! A 2D square gets squashed flat into a 1D line or 0D point (Area $= 0$).

<Analogy>
  If matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is a camera lens zooming a photo: $|\det(\mathbf{A})| = 4$ zooms $4\times$ larger in area, $|\det(\mathbf{A})| = 0.25$ shrinks to a quarter area, and $\det(\mathbf{A}) = 0$ crushes the entire 2D photo into a 1-pixel-thin line—deleting pixel information forever!
</Analogy>

### 3. Matrix Inverse ($\mathbf{A}^{-1}$): The Exact Undo Button

For a regular non-zero scalar like $5$, multiplying by $\frac{1}{5}$ returns $1$. For a square matrix $\mathbf{A}$, its **inverse** $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ satisfies:

$$
\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \mathbf{I}
$$

![Matrix inverse A^-1 reverses A when det is non-zero, but is impossible when det equals zero](/images/transpose-inverse-determinant/03_inverse_undo_machine.png)

#### Explicit $2 \times 2$ Inverse Formula
Swap the main diagonal ($a \leftrightarrow d$), negate the off-diagonal ($-b, -c$), and divide by $\det(\mathbf{A}) = ad - bc$:

$$
\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}
$$

Notice immediately why $\det(\mathbf{A}) = 0$ breaks everything: **you cannot divide by zero!** Geometrically, once $\mathbf{A}$ crushes 2D space onto a 1D line, multiple inputs land on the exact same spot—no function can possibly tell which original point came from where, so **singular matrices have no inverse**.

Worked example with $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(\mathbf{A}) &= 3(2) - 1(2) = 4 \\
\mathbf{A}^{-1} &= \frac{1}{4} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -0.25 \\ -0.5 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check that multiplying $\mathbf{A}$ by $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ returns the identity:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1} &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -0.25 \\ -0.5 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{I}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Why ML Engineers Prefer `solve(A, b)` Over `inv(A) @ b`
Computing an explicit inverse $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ is slow ($\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ with large constant overhead) and magnifies floating-point rounding noise whenever $\det(\mathbf{A})$ is tiny. Solving $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ directly via **LU decomposition** (`np.linalg.solve(A, b)`) is both faster and numerically cleaner!

---

### 4. Singular vs Non-Singular, Rank, and Null Space

Every square matrix falls into one of two camps:
- **Non-Singular (Invertible)**: $\det(\mathbf{A}) \neq 0$, full rank, no dimensions crushed, $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ exists.
- **Singular (Non-Invertible)**: $\det(\mathbf{A}) = 0$, at least one redundant column collapses a dimension flat, $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ does not exist.

This leads directly to two fundamental subspaces for *any* $m \times n$ matrix $\mathbf{A}$:
- **Rank ($\text{rank}(\mathbf{A})$)**: Number of linearly independent columns (equals the number of surviving dimensions in the **Column Space** output).
- **Null Space (Kernel)**: Set of all input vectors $\mathbf{x}$ flattened straight to zero ($\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$). Its dimension is the **nullity**. A matrix is non-singular if and only if its null space contains *only* the trivial zero vector $\mathbf{0}$!

![Diagram showing Rank as surviving Column Space dimensions and Null Space as directions crushed to zero](/images/transpose-inverse-determinant/04_rank_and_nullspace.png)

$$
\text{rank}(\mathbf{A}) + \text{nullity}(\mathbf{A}) = n
$$

where $n$ is the total number of columns (features) of $\mathbf{A}$.

  $\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$

<Formula label="Key Algebraic Identities">
  $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^T = \mathbf{B}^T\mathbf{A}^T$
  <br />
  $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^{-1} = \mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}$
  <br />
  $\det(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}) = \det(\mathbf{A})\det(\mathbf{B})$
</Formula>
