<Section label="What is it?">

**Linear Regression** is a fundamental supervised machine learning algorithm that models a linear relationship between one or more input variables, called **features** ($X$), and a continuous numerical output, called the **target** ($Y$).

It is widely used to predict continuous real-world quantities such as exam scores, house prices, salaries, temperatures, and sales volumes.

### A Motivating Example
Suppose we want to predict a student's final exam score ($Y$) based on the number of hours they spent studying ($X$):

| Study Hours ($X$) | Exam Score ($Y$) |
| :---: | :---: |
| 1 | 20 |
| 2 | 35 |
| 3 | 45 |
| 4 | 60 |
| 5 | 75 |
| 6 | 85 |

The input is study hours ($X$), and the target is exam score ($Y$). The goal of linear regression is not to pass through every single observed point, but rather to discover a line that best captures the overall trend of the data to make accurate predictions for new, unseen study hours.

![Observed data and an illustrative fitted regression line](/images/linear-regression/linear_regression_fit.png)

</Section>

<Section label="Why we need it">

Almost every quantitative system in science, engineering, and business seeks to understand how changes in an input produce changes in an output:
- **Education Analytics**: How much does an extra hour of study boost exam performance?
- **Healthcare**: How does medication dosage correlate with blood pressure reduction?
- **Economics & Finance**: How do interest rates impact market returns?

Linear regression is the universal benchmark of predictive modeling because:
1. **Direct Interpretability**: Each learned parameter has clear physical meaning.
2. **Transparent Mechanics**: You can trace exactly how predictions are calculated and why errors occur.
3. **Foundation of Deep Learning**: A single neuron in an artificial neural network is essentially a linear regression unit followed by an activation function!

</Section>

### 1. The Linear Regression Equation

For simple linear regression with one feature, the prediction equation is:

$$
\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x
$$

This shares the exact same mathematical structure as the classic slope-intercept equation $y = mx + c$:
- $x$ is the input feature (e.g., study hours).
- $\hat{y}$ (pronounced *y-hat*) is the model's predicted output.
- $\beta_0$ (beta-zero) is the **y-intercept**.
- $\beta_1$ (beta-one) is the **slope** (or coefficient).

#### Understanding the Intercept ($\beta_0$)
The intercept is the model's predicted output when the input is zero:
$$
\hat{y}\big|_{x=0} = \beta_0
$$
For example, in $\hat{y} = 10 + 12x$, the intercept is $10$. When a student studies $0$ hours, the model predicts a score of $10$.

#### Understanding the Slope ($\beta_1$)
The slope describes the change in predicted output for a one-unit increase in the input:
$$
\Delta \hat{y} = \beta_1 \Delta x
$$
If $\beta_1 = 12$, increasing study time by one hour increases the predicted score by $12$ points. Note that this describes the model's learned association, not necessarily a direct causal guarantee.

![Positive, negative, and zero slope examples](/images/linear-regression/slope_types.png)

- **$\beta_1 > 0$ (Positive slope)**: As study hours increase, predicted score increases.
- **$\beta_1 < 0$ (Negative slope)**: As input increases, predicted output decreases (e.g., car age vs. resale value).
- **$\beta_1 = 0$ (Zero slope)**: The input has no linear predictive power over the target; the line is flat.

<Callout kind="tip">
  **Statistical Convention: Beta vs. Beta-Hat**
  In statistics, $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ represent the true (unknown) underlying population parameters. The numbers estimated from sample training data are written with a hat: $\hat{\beta}_0$ and $\hat{\beta}_1$.
</Callout>

### 2. Measuring Prediction Errors: Residuals

Suppose we test an initial, imperfect model:
$$
\hat{y} = 10 + 10x
$$

Let's compare its predictions against our training data:

| $X$ | Actual $Y$ | Predicted $\hat{Y}$ | Residual $e = Y - \hat{Y}$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 1 | 25 | 20 | $+5$ |
| 2 | 40 | 30 | $+10$ |
| 3 | 50 | 40 | $+10$ |
| 4 | 65 | 50 | $+15$ |
| 5 | 75 | 60 | $+15$ |

A **residual** $e_i$ is the vertical difference between the true observed value and the model's prediction:

$$
e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i
$$

![Residuals are vertical distances between observations and predictions](/images/linear-regression/residuals.png)

- **Positive residual ($e_i > 0$)**: The actual point lies *above* the line (the model underpredicted).
- **Negative residual ($e_i < 0$)**: The actual point lies *below* the line (the model overpredicted).
- **Zero residual ($e_i = 0$)**: The line passes directly through the point.

#### Why Not Simply Sum the Residuals?
If an error is $+10$ and another is $-10$, adding them yields:
$$
+10 + (-10) = 0
$$
Positive and negative residuals cancel out! A total error sum of zero would trick the algorithm into thinking a dreadful model is completely flawless.

---

### 3. Cost Functions: RSS and MSE

To prevent positive and negative errors from canceling, we square each residual.

#### Residual Sum of Squares (RSS)
$$
\text{RSS} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2
$$
For residuals $[+5, +10, -5, -10]$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{RSS} &= 5^2 + 10^2 + (-5)^2 + (-10)^2 \\
&= 25 + 100 + 25 + 100 = 250
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Mean Squared Error (MSE)
Dividing RSS by the number of training examples $n$ gives the average squared penalty, known as the **Mean Squared Error (MSE)** cost function:

$$
J(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - (\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i))^2
$$

The learning objective of linear regression is to find the parameters $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ that minimize this cost function:
$$
\min_{\beta_0, \beta_1} J(\beta_0, \beta_1)
$$

---

### 4. How Does the Model Learn? Gradient Descent

Imagine walking down a misty mountain in dense fog. You cannot see the lowest valley at the bottom. What do you do? At your current position, you feel the slope beneath your feet, take a step in the steepest downhill direction, and repeat until the ground flattens out.

![Illustration of iterative steps moving toward a cost minimum](/images/linear-regression/gradient_descent_steps.png)

In machine learning optimization:
- The **height** of the mountain is your cost ($J$).
- Your **coordinates** on the mountain are your parameters ($\beta_0, \beta_1$).
- The **negative gradient** ($-\nabla J$) tells you which direction leads downhill.

#### The Update Rule
The general Gradient Descent update rule is:
$$
\theta_{\text{new}} = \theta_{\text{old}} - \alpha \frac{\partial J}{\partial \theta}
$$
where $\alpha$ is the **learning rate** (step size).

![Illustrative parameter trajectories for different learning rates](/images/linear-regression/learning_rate_comparison.png)

- **$\alpha$ too small**: Steps are minuscule; training takes thousands of iterations to converge.
- **$\alpha$ well-chosen**: Steps progress smoothly and efficiently toward the minimum.
- **$\alpha$ too large**: Steps overshoot the valley floor, oscillating wildly or even diverging to infinity.

#### The Gradients for Simple Linear Regression
Applying the calculus chain rule to the MSE cost function yields two partial derivatives:

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial \beta_0} = \frac{2}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)
$$

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial \beta_1} = \frac{2}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - y_i) x_i
$$

At each iteration, we update both parameters simultaneously:
$$
\beta_0 \leftarrow \beta_0 - \alpha \frac{\partial J}{\partial \beta_0}
$$
$$
\beta_1 \leftarrow \beta_1 - \alpha \frac{\partial J}{\partial \beta_1}
$$

---

### 5. A Complete Worked Numerical Example

Let's trace one complete update step by hand with a single training point:
- Feature: $x = 2$
- Target: $y = 6$
- Initial guesses: $\beta_0 = 0$, $\beta_1 = 1$
- Learning rate: $\alpha = 0.1$

**Step 1: Compute Prediction**
$$
\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x = 0 + 1(2) = 2
$$

**Step 2: Calculate Gradients (for $n = 1$)**
$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial \beta_0} = 2(\hat{y} - y) = 2(2 - 6) = -8
$$
$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial \beta_1} = 2(\hat{y} - y) x = 2(2 - 6)(2) = -16
$$

**Step 3: Update the Intercept ($\beta_0$)**
$$
\beta_0 = 0 - 0.1(-8) = 0 + 0.8 = \mathbf{0.8}
$$

**Step 4: Update the Slope ($\beta_1$)**
$$
\beta_1 = 1 - 0.1(-16) = 1 + 1.6 = \mathbf{2.6}
$$

**Resulting Updated Model:**
$$
\hat{y} = 0.8 + 2.6x
$$
Testing our new model on $x = 2$:
$$
\hat{y} = 0.8 + 2.6(2) = 6.0
$$
The squared error plummeted from $(6 - 2)^2 = 16$ down to $(6 - 6)^2 = 0$!

<Formula label="Prediction Equation (Simple Linear Regression)">
  $\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$
</Formula>

<Formula label="Residual Definition">
  $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$

<Formula label="Mean Squared Error (MSE Cost Function)">
  $J(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( y_i - (\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i) \right)^2$
</Formula>

<Formula label="Gradient Descent Parameter Updates">
  $\beta_0 \leftarrow \beta_0 - \alpha \left[ \frac{2}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - y_i) \right], \quad \beta_1 \leftarrow \beta_1 - \alpha \left[ \frac{2}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - y_i) x_i \right]$
</Formula>

<Callout kind="tip">
  **Simultaneous Updates**: When implementing Gradient Descent in code, always calculate both gradients $\frac{\partial J}{\partial \beta_0}$ and $\frac{\partial J}{\partial \beta_1}$ using the current parameter values before updating either parameter.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning">
  **The Cancellation Trap**: Never minimize raw residuals $\sum e_i$. Because positive and negative errors cancel out, squaring $(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ is strictly required to ensure every mistake is penalized.
</Callout>
