### 1. The True Model in Matrix Form

In multiple linear regression, we relate a continuous dependent target variable $y$ to $k$ explanatory feature variables across $n$ observations. In scalar summation notation, this equation quickly becomes unwieldy. Matrix algebra provides a clean, rigorous, and computationally efficient representation.

The population regression model in matrix notation is:

$$
\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
$$

Let's break down each component and its exact dimensions:

- **$\mathbf{X}$ is an $n \times k$ Design Matrix**: Contains the observations on $k$ independent variables across $n$ training instances. When the model includes an intercept (constant term), the first column of $\mathbf{X}$ consists entirely of ones ($\mathbf{1}$).
- **$\mathbf{y}$ is an $n \times 1$ Vector**: The observed values of the dependent target variable.
- **$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ is an $n \times 1$ Vector of Disturbances**: The unobserved random errors or influences not captured by the features.
- **$\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is a $k \times 1$ Vector of True Parameters**: The fixed but unknown population coefficients we wish to estimate.

The model decomposes into a **systematic component** ($\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}$) and a **stochastic component** ($\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$). Our goal in estimation is to infer the unknown population vector $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ using sample observations.

#### Matrix Dimensions Check
Matrix dimensions must align under standard matrix multiplication:

$$
(n \times k) \times (k \times 1) = n \times 1
$$

Thus, $\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}$ has dimensions $n \times 1$, which matches the dimension of $\mathbf{y}$ and $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$.

![Dimension check for the scalar product y'X beta-hat](/images/ordinary-least-squares/matrix_dimensions.png)

---

### 2. Estimates, Fitted Values, and Residuals

Let $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ denote the estimated parameter vector calculated from our sample data. 

The **fitted (predicted) values** $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ are obtained by projecting the design matrix through our parameter estimate:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
$$

The **residual vector** $\mathbf{e}$ is the sample difference between the actual observations and the fitted predictions:

$$
\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
$$

#### Disturbances ($\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$) vs. Residuals ($\mathbf{e}$)
Students often conflate the theoretical error term with the empirical residual. They represent fundamentally different concepts:

| Property | Disturbance $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ | Residual $\mathbf{e}$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Meaning** | Unobserved noise in the true population model | Observed minus fitted sample prediction |
| **Observable?** | **No** (theoretical population quantity) | **Yes** (calculated directly from sample data) |
| **Role** | Part of the data-generating process | Generated after estimating $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ |

<Callout kind="warning">
  **Crucial Distinction**: In general, $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \ne \mathbf{e}$. Never infer properties of the unobserved population disturbances solely because sample residuals happen to satisfy mathematical identities by construction!
</Callout>

---

### 3. The OLS Criterion: Minimizing the Sum of Squared Residuals (RSS)

The Ordinary Least Squares criterion selects the estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ that minimizes the scalar sum of squared residuals:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{RSS} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2 \\
&= \mathbf{e}^T\mathbf{e}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Why Does $\sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2 = \mathbf{e}^T\mathbf{e}$?

To see why the sum of squared residuals equals the vector dot product $\mathbf{e}^T\mathbf{e}$, write out the vectors explicitly:

1. **Residual Column Vector** ($\mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 1}$):
   $$
   \mathbf{e} = \begin{bmatrix} e_1 \\ \vdots \\ e_n \end{bmatrix}
   $$

2. **Transposed Row Vector** ($\mathbf{e}^T \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times n}$):
   $$
   \mathbf{e}^T = \begin{bmatrix} e_1 & \dots & e_n \end{bmatrix}
   $$

3. **Inner Product (Row $\times$ Column)**:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \mathbf{e}^T\mathbf{e} &= \begin{bmatrix} e_1 & \dots & e_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} e_1 \\ \vdots \\ e_n \end{bmatrix} \\
   &= e_1^2 + \dots + e_n^2 \\
   &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2
   \end{aligned}
   $$

In geometric terms, $\mathbf{e}^T\mathbf{e} = \|\mathbf{e}\|_2^2$ is the **squared Euclidean norm** (length) of the residual vector in observation space $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Substituting the residual definition $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$:

$$
\text{RSS} = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
$$

Recall the transpose property for matrix products and differences:
$$
(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^T = \mathbf{B}^T\mathbf{A}^T, \qquad (\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B})^T = \mathbf{A}^T - \mathbf{B}^T
$$

Applying this to our residual expression:
$$
(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^T = \mathbf{y}^T - (\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^T = \mathbf{y}^T - \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T
$$

Substituting this back into RSS and distributing terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{RSS} &= (\mathbf{y}^T - \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T)(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) \\
&= \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} \\
&\quad + \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Why is $\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ a Scalar?
Let's verify the dimensions of the middle term:
$$
\underbrace{\mathbf{y}^T}_{1 \times n} \times \underbrace{\mathbf{X}}_{n \times k} \times \underbrace{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k \times 1}
$$
Multiplying from left to right:
$$
(1 \times n) \times (n \times k) = 1 \times k
$$
$$
(1 \times k) \times (k \times 1) = 1 \times 1
$$

A $1 \times 1$ matrix is a single real number (scalar). Because every scalar equals its own transpose:

$$
\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^T = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}
$$

The two cross-terms in the expansion are identical! Combining them yields the **Master Expanded RSS**:

$$
\text{RSS} = \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y} - 2\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
$$

---

### 4. Differentiating RSS with Respect to $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$

To find the coefficient vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ that minimizes RSS, we take the vector derivative of RSS with respect to $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ and equate it to the zero vector.

#### Useful Matrix Differentiation Rules
1. **Linear Form**: For constant vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and variable vector $\mathbf{b}$:
   $$
   \frac{\partial (\mathbf{b}^T\mathbf{a})}{\partial \mathbf{b}} = \mathbf{a}
   $$
2. **Quadratic Form**: For a square matrix $\mathbf{A}$:
   $$
   \frac{\partial (\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{A}^T)\boldsymbol{\beta}
   $$
   If $\mathbf{A}$ is **symmetric** ($\mathbf{A}^T = \mathbf{A}$), this simplifies to:
   $$
   \frac{\partial (\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} = 2\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta}
   $$

#### Deriving the Quadratic-Form Rule via Differentials
Let $f(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta}$. Using the product rule in differential form:
$$
df = d\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{A}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$
Because the second term is a scalar, it equals its transpose:
$$
\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{A}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta} = (\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{A}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta})^T = d\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{A}^T\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$
Factoring out $d\boldsymbol{\beta}^T$:
$$
df = d\boldsymbol{\beta}^T(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{A}^T)\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$
Therefore, the gradient is $(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{A}^T)\boldsymbol{\beta}$.

#### Differentiating the Three RSS Terms
Differentiating each term of $\text{RSS} = \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y} - 2\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$:

1. **Term 1 ($\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y}$)**: Does not depend on $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$:
   $$
   \frac{\partial (\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y})}{\partial \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}} = \mathbf{0}
   $$
2. **Term 2 ($-2\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$)**: Applying the linear rule where $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$:
   $$
   \frac{\partial (-2\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y})}{\partial \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}} = -2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}
   $$
3. **Term 3 ($\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$)**: Here $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$. Since $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^T = \mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{X}^T)^T = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$, the matrix is symmetric:
   $$
   \frac{\partial (\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}} = 2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
   $$

Combining all three derivatives gives the **RSS Gradient Vector**:

$$
\frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}} = -2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + 2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
$$

---

### 5. Normal Equations and the OLS Estimator

At the minimum, the gradient must equal the zero vector:

$$
-2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + 2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{0}
$$

Dividing by $2$ and rearranging gives the celebrated **Normal Equations**:

$$
\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}
$$

If the $k \times k$ matrix $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is invertible (non-singular), we premultiply both sides by $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}$:

$$
(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}
$$

Since $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{I}_k$, we arrive at the **Closed-Form OLS Estimator**:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}
$$

#### When Does $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ Fail to Exist?
The matrix $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is non-invertible if:
1. **$n < k$ (Underdetermined system)**: Fewer observations than independent features.
2. **Perfect Multicollinearity**: One or more columns of $\mathbf{X}$ are exact linear combinations of other columns (e.g., redundant or duplicate features).

The **full column rank condition** ($\text{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = k$) guarantees that $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is strictly positive definite and invertible.

---

### 6. Verifying the Stationary Point is a Minimum

To confirm that the stationary point is a strict local minimum rather than a maximum or saddle point, we evaluate the second derivative (Hessian matrix):

$$
\frac{\partial^2 \text{RSS}}{\partial \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \partial \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^T} = 2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}
$$

If $\mathbf{X}$ has full column rank, then for any non-zero vector $\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}$:
$$
\mathbf{v}^T (2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}) \mathbf{v} = 2(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v})^T(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}) = 2 \|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}\|^2 > 0
$$

Since the quadratic form is strictly positive for all non-zero vectors, the Hessian matrix $2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is **positive definite**. Consequently, the stationary point $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ is guaranteed to be a unique global minimum.

---

### 7. Fundamental Property: Residual Orthogonality ($\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$)

Starting from the normal equations $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$ and substituting the residual definition $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{e} &= \mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) \\
&= \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \\
&= \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} \\
&= \mathbf{0}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}
$$

This equation demonstrates that the residual vector $\mathbf{e}$ is **strictly orthogonal to every single column of the design matrix $\mathbf{X}$**!

![Conceptual illustration of residual orthogonality. The residual direction is perpendicular to the column-space direction.](/images/ordinary-least-squares/residual_orthogonality.png)

#### Column-by-Column Interpretation
If $\mathbf{x}_j$ is the $j$-th column of $\mathbf{X}$, then $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$ implies:

$$
\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{e} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{ij} e_i = 0 \qquad \text{for every feature } j = 1, \dots, k
$$

In sample terms, every regressor has zero sample correlation with the OLS residuals.

#### Consequences When the Model Includes an Intercept
When the regression includes a constant intercept term, the first column of $\mathbf{X}$ is a column of ones: $\mathbf{1} = [1, 1, \dots, 1]^T$.

Evaluating the first element of $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$:

$$
\mathbf{1}^T\mathbf{e} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i = 0
$$

The sum of OLS residuals is always zero! Dividing by the sample size $n$ gives the sample mean of residuals:

$$
\bar{e} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i = 0
$$

<Callout kind="tip">
  **Note on Intercept Requirement**: The properties $\sum e_i = 0$ and $\bar{e} = 0$ hold **only** when the model includes an intercept column. In regressions forced through the origin without an intercept, the residuals generally do not sum to zero!
</Callout>
