### 1. The Big Picture: What is Lasso Regression?

In machine learning and statistics, we often encounter datasets with dozens or hundreds of candidate features, many of which may be irrelevant, noisy, or redundant. 

While Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) assigns a non-zero weight to every available feature, and Ridge Regression shrinks coefficients toward zero without ever discarding any, **Lasso Regression** (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) accomplishes two tasks simultaneously:
1. **Regularization**: It prevents overfitting by shrinking coefficient magnitudes.
2. **Feature Selection**: It drives uninformative or redundant coefficients to **exactly zero**, producing a sparse, interpretable model.

The Lasso objective function is:

$$
J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( y_i - \sum_{j=1}^{p} X_{ij}\beta_j \right)^2}_{\text{RSS (Data Mismatch)}} + \underbrace{\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j|}_{L_1\text{ Penalty (Sparsity)}}
$$

In matrix notation:

$$
J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_1
$$

where $\lambda \ge 0$ is the regularization tuning parameter.

| Penalty Level | Value of $\lambda$ | Model Behavior |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **No Regularization** | $\lambda = 0$ | Penalty vanishes; equivalent to unregularized **OLS**. |
| **Moderate Regularization** | $\lambda > 0$ | Weak features are pruned to exact zero; informative features are shrunk. |
| **Maximum Threshold** | $\lambda \ge \lambda_{\max}$ | The penalty is so dominant that **all coefficients become zero** (null model). |

### 2. The $L_1$ Penalty and Geometric Intuition

To understand why Lasso produces exact zeros while Ridge does not, we examine the problem from a **constrained optimization** perspective (Lagrangian duality):

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \text{RSS}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j| \le s
$$

where $s$ is an allowable coefficient budget inversely related to $\lambda$.

![Comparison between L1 diamond geometry and L2 circular geometry](/images/lasso-regression/l1_l2_geometry.png)

In 2D parameter space $(\beta_1, \beta_2)$:
- **The OLS Contours (Blue Ellipses)**: Centered at the unconstrained minimum $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{OLS}}$. Each ellipse traces parameter combinations that share identical Residual Sum of Squares.
- **The $L_2$ Constraint (Ridge Circle)**: The constraint $\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 \le s$ is a smooth sphere/circle. The expanding ellipse touches the circle along its smooth curved edge, retaining non-zero values for both parameters.
- **The $L_1$ Constraint (Lasso Diamond)**: The constraint $|\beta_1| + |\beta_2| \le s$ is a **rotated square (diamond)** whose sharp corners (vertices) lie precisely on the coordinate axes:
  $$(\pm s, 0) \quad \text{and} \quad (0, \pm s)$$

#### Why Corners Cause Exact Zeros
Because the diamond constraint has sharp pointy corners aligned with the coordinate axes, the expanding elliptical RSS contours have a very high mathematical probability of first making contact with the boundary at a **corner**! 

When contact occurs at the corner $(s, 0)$, parameter $\beta_2$ is **strictly zero**:
$$
\hat{\beta}_1 = s, \qquad \hat{\beta}_2 = 0
$$

Feature $x_2$ is completely pruned from the model!

---

### 3. Optimization: Why Doesn't Lasso Have a Matrix Inverse?

Students often ask: *Why can we invert a matrix to solve Ridge $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{Ridge}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$, but cannot do the same for Lasso?*

The answer lies in calculus. The absolute value function $|x|$ has a sharp, non-differentiable corner (kink) at $x = 0$:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}|x| = \begin{cases} +1, & x > 0 \\ -1, & x < 0 \\ \text{undefined}, & x = 0 \end{cases}
$$

Because the derivative does not exist at zero, we cannot differentiate the objective using standard calculus to set up a linear system of equations.

#### Subgradients
For non-differentiable convex functions, we generalize the derivative using **subgradients**. A scalar $g$ is a subgradient of a convex function $f$ at point $x$ if the line with slope $g$ passing through $(x, f(x))$ lies everywhere on or below the graph of $f$.

For the absolute value function $f(x) = |x|$, the subdifferential is:

$$
\partial |x| = \begin{cases} \{+1\}, & x > 0 \\ [-1, 1], & x = 0 \\ \{-1\}, & x < 0 \end{cases}
$$

At the sharp corner $x = 0$, **any slope between $-1$ and $+1$** is a valid subgradient! This continuous interval $[-1, 1]$ is the exact mathematical mechanism that allows solutions to lock onto zero across a wide range of data configurations.

The subgradient optimality condition for Lasso is:

$$
\mathbf{0} \in 2\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbf{y}) + \lambda \partial \|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_1
$$

---

### 4. Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) Optimality Conditions

Let $\mathbf{x}_j$ denote the $j$-th column of $\mathbf{X}$, and let $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ denote the final residual vector. The $j$-th coordinate condition from the subgradient equation is:

$$
0 \in -2\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{r} + \lambda \partial |\hat{\beta}_j|
$$

Evaluating the three cases of the subdifferential $\partial |\hat{\beta}_j|$ yields the **Coordinate-wise KKT Conditions**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{r} &= \lambda, &\quad &\text{if } \hat{\beta}_j > 0 \\
2\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{r} &= -\lambda, &\quad &\text{if } \hat{\beta}_j < 0 \\
|2\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{r}| &\le \lambda, &\quad &\text{if } \hat{\beta}_j = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

<Callout kind="tip">
  **The KKT Threshold Intuition**: For a feature to have a non-zero coefficient, its sample correlation with the residual ($2\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{r}$) must hit the upper or lower boundary $\pm \lambda$. If its correlation falls within the interior deadband $[-\lambda, \lambda]$, the feature is not informative enough to justify its $L_1$ penalty cost, and its coefficient remains locked at **exact zero**!
</Callout>

---

### 5. The Soft-Thresholding Operator

When predictors are orthogonal ($\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{I}$), the multivariate Lasso problem uncouples into $p$ independent scalar minimization problems. Each coordinate solution is given by the **Soft-Thresholding Operator**:

$$
\mathcal{S}(z, t) = \text{sign}(z) \max(|z| - t, 0)
$$

Equivalently written piecewise:

$$
\mathcal{S}(z, t) = \begin{cases} z - t, & z > t \\ 0, & -t \le z \le t \\ z + t, & z < -t \end{cases}
$$

![Soft-thresholding operator with threshold t = 2 showing the deadband where values are zeroed](/images/lasso-regression/soft_thresholding.png)

Notice how soft-thresholding operates:
1. **Deadband $[-t, t]$**: Any input whose magnitude is less than or equal to $t$ is snapped strictly to zero!
2. **Outside the Deadband**: Inputs exceeding $t$ in magnitude are preserved, but shrunk toward zero by a constant offset of $t$.

---

### 6. The Coordinate Descent Algorithm

Because we cannot solve for all $p$ coefficients simultaneously in one matrix inversion, how do modern libraries (like `scikit-learn` and `glmnet`) solve Lasso? They use **Coordinate Descent**.

![Flowchart of the coordinate descent optimization loop](/images/lasso-regression/coordinate_descent_flow.png)

#### The Core Idea
Instead of updating all parameters at once, Coordinate Descent minimizes the objective with respect to **one coordinate $\beta_j$ at a time**, while keeping all other $p - 1$ coordinates fixed at their current values.

Let $\mathbf{r}_j$ be the **partial residual** calculated by removing the effect of feature $j$:

$$
\mathbf{r}_j = \mathbf{y} - \sum_{k \ne j} \mathbf{x}_k \beta_k = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{x}_j \beta_j
$$

The single-coordinate subproblem reduces to:

$$
\min_{\beta_j} \|\mathbf{r}_j - \mathbf{x}_j \beta_j\|_2^2 + \lambda |\beta_j|
$$

This univariate problem has a closed-form solution given directly by soft-thresholding!

#### The Coordinate Update Formula
$$
\beta_j^{\text{new}} = \frac{\mathcal{S}\left(\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{r}_j, \, \frac{\lambda}{2}\right)}{\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{x}_j}
$$

When features are standardized so that $\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{x}_j = 1$, the update simplifies to:

$$
\beta_j^{\text{new}} = \mathcal{S}\left(\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{r}_j, \, \frac{\lambda}{2}\right)
$$

#### Numerical Example
Suppose for feature $j$, $\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{r}_j = 7$, $\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{x}_j = 4$, and penalty $\lambda = 2$ (so threshold $t = \lambda/2 = 1$):
$$
\mathcal{S}(7, 1) = 7 - 1 = 6 \implies \beta_j^{\text{new}} = \frac{6}{4} = 1.5
$$
If instead the correlation was weak, say $\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{r}_j = 0.6$:
$$
\mathcal{S}(0.6, 1) = 0 \implies \beta_j^{\text{new}} = 0.0
$$
The coefficient is instantly pruned!

The algorithm repeatedly cycles through coordinates $j = 1, 2, \dots, p$ until the maximum coefficient change falls below a convergence tolerance $\epsilon$ (e.g., $10^{-4}$).

### 7. Practical Modeling: Scaling and Choosing $\lambda$

#### Mandatory Feature Standardization
Because the $L_1$ penalty sums the raw absolute values $\sum |\beta_j|$, features measured in large units (e.g., house price in millions vs. number of bathrooms) will have arbitrarily small coefficients and escape penalization.

Always standardize features using training set statistics:

$$
z_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j}{s_j}
$$

#### The All-Zero Threshold ($\lambda_{\max}$)
At what penalty value do all coefficients become zero? Using the KKT conditions at $\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0}$, every coordinate condition $|2\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{y}| \le \lambda$ must hold. Thus:

$$
\lambda_{\max} = 2 \max_{j=1,\dots,p} |\mathbf{x}_j^T\mathbf{y}|
$$

Any $\lambda \ge \lambda_{\max}$ yields the trivial null model $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{0}$. Effective grids for hyperparameter search typically span logarithmically from $\lambda_{\max}$ down to $10^{-4}\lambda_{\max}$.

#### Cross-Validation Workflow
We select the optimal $\lambda$ using $K$-Fold Cross-Validation:

![Five-fold cross-validation workflow: training on 4 folds, testing on 1 holdout](/images/lasso-regression/five_fold_cv.png)

![Regularization path showing coefficients dropping to zero one by one](/images/lasso-regression/regularization_path.png)

1. **Regularization Path**: As $\lambda$ is decreased from $\lambda_{\max}$, features enter the active set one by one.
2. **$\lambda_{\text{min}}$ vs $\lambda_{\text{1se}}$**: We can select the $\lambda$ with the absolute lowest cross-validation error ($\lambda_{\text{min}}$), or use the **1-Standard-Error rule** ($\lambda_{\text{1se}}$)—the simplest (sparsest) model whose error is within one standard error of the minimum.

### 8. Strengths & Limitations of Lasso

#### Strengths
- **Interpretability**: Prunes irrelevant features, leaving a compact, readable formula.
- **High-Dimensional Regression ($p > n$)**: When there are more features than observations, OLS is undefined. Lasso can select at most $n$ predictors and provides stable estimates.

#### Limitations & Motivation for Elastic Net
1. **Correlated Predictor Selection**: When two features are strongly collinear ($r \approx 0.99$), Lasso tends to arbitrarily select one and zero out the other.
2. **Constraint on Active Set**: If $p > n$, Lasso can never select more than $n$ variables.
3. **Over-Shrinkage of Large Slopes**: Because the penalty subtracts a constant offset $t$ from all surviving coefficients, true large signals may suffer excess bias.

These limitations motivate **Elastic Net**, which combines both $L_1$ (Lasso) and $L_2$ (Ridge) penalties: $\lambda_1 \|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_1 + \lambda_2 \|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2$.

### Summary Reference Table

| Feature / Property | Ordinary Least Squares | Ridge ($L_2$) | Lasso ($L_1$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Penalty Term** | None | $\lambda \sum \beta_j^2$ | $\lambda \sum |\beta_j|$ |
| **Constraint Shape** | None | Smooth circle / sphere | Sharp diamond / octahedron |
| **Analytical Solution** | Yes: $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$ | Yes: $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$ | No closed-form (solved via Coordinate Descent) |
| **Differentiability** | Everywhere differentiable | Everywhere differentiable | Non-differentiable at $\beta_j = 0$ (uses Subgradients) |
| **Feature Selection** | No (all features non-zero) | No (shrinks close to zero, never exact zero) | **Yes** (drives coefficients to exact zero) |
| **Core Optimizer** | Normal equations | Linear matrix inversion | **Coordinate Descent** & Soft-Thresholding |
| **Handling $p > n$** | Fails (singular matrix) | Works (stabilizes matrix) | Works (selects at most $n$ features) |
| **Feature Scaling** | Optional | **Mandatory** | **Mandatory** |
