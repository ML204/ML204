### 1. The Big Picture & Motivation

In standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Linear Regression, we enforce a strict structural constraint: the relationship between predictor $x$ and continuous response $y$ must be a straight line:

$$
y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)
$$

This constraint dictates that the marginal rate of change is constant everywhere: $\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} = \beta_1$. Whether $x = 1$ or $x = 1{,}000$, a 1-unit increase in $x$ produces the exact same expected change in $y$.

However, natural, physical, and socio-economic systems rarely follow rigid straight lines:
- **Kinetic Physics:** The braking distance of an automobile grows quadratically with vehicle velocity ($d \propto v^2$).
- **Economics:** Diminishing marginal utility, where each additional dollar of capital yields progressively smaller marginal output.
- **Pharmacology:** Dose-response curves, where drug efficacy rises rapidly, plateaus, and eventually becomes toxic at high dosages.
- **Education & Cognition:** Exam scores versus study hours, where initial study hours provide substantial gains followed by fatigue and performance saturation.

When we fit a straight line to curved data, the model suffers from severe **systematic bias** (underfitting). The residuals display distinct, non-random U-shaped or cyclical patterns rather than uncorrelated white noise.

![Comparison of Linear Regression vs Polynomial Regression on nonlinear data](/images/polynomial-regression/01_linear_vs_nonlinear.png)

<Callout kind="tip">
  **Core Motivation**: Polynomial Regression broadens the hypothesis class of linear models to fit nonlinear curves by adding powers of original predictors ($x^2, x^3, \dots, x^d$) as new input features, while keeping the estimation problem entirely linear with respect to the unknown weights $\boldsymbol{\beta}$.
</Callout>

### 2. What Exactly is a Polynomial?

A polynomial in a single real variable $x$ is a mathematical expression composed of variables and coefficients involving only addition, subtraction, multiplication, and non-negative integer exponents:

$$
P(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \dots + \beta_d x^d = \sum_{j=0}^d \beta_j x^j
$$

The highest exponent $d \in \mathbb{N}_0$ associated with a non-zero coefficient $\beta_d \ne 0$ is called the **degree** of the polynomial.

- **Degree 0 (Constant):** $y = \beta_0$ — a horizontal flat line (0 turning points).
- **Degree 1 (Linear):** $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$ — a sloped straight line (0 turning points).
- **Degree 2 (Quadratic):** $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2$ — a parabola with exactly 1 turning point (vertex / extremum).
- **Degree 3 (Cubic):** $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3$ — an S-shaped curve with 1 inflection point and up to 2 turning points.
- **Degree 4 (Quartic):** $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \dots + \beta_4 x^4$ — a W or M-shaped curve with up to 3 turning points.

![Geometric behavior of polynomial functions across degrees 1 to 4](/images/polynomial-regression/02_polynomial_degrees.png)

<Analogy>
  **The Flexible Ruler Analogy**: Think of polynomial degree $d$ as the number of flexible joints in a carpenter's folding ruler. A ruler with 1 segment (degree 1) can only be laid flat as a straight line. Adding joints allows it to bend at 1 point (quadratic), 2 points (cubic), or wiggle through complex shapes. However, too many joints make the ruler floppy and hypersensitive to tiny bumps (overfitting).
</Analogy>

A polynomial of degree $d$ can have at most **$d - 1$ turning points** (local extrema where the first derivative $\frac{dP}{dx} = 0$). Increasing degree $d$ directly dials the mathematical flexibility of the curve.

### 3. The Fundamental Definition of Linearity in Machine Learning

A common point of confusion for students is why Polynomial Regression is universally classified as a **linear model** when its graphical prediction curve is obviously curved.

<Callout kind="warning">
  **The Golden Rule of Linearity**: In statistical machine learning, a model is defined as **linear** if it is linear in its **parameters (coefficients) $\boldsymbol{\beta}$**, regardless of how nonlinear the feature transformations $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ may be!
</Callout>

Consider the univariate quadratic model:

$$
y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \varepsilon
$$

Let us define synthesized feature variables:

$$
z_1 = x, \qquad z_2 = x^2
$$

Rewriting the equation with these transformed features yields:

$$
y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 z_1 + \beta_2 z_2 + \varepsilon
$$

This is identical to a standard multiple linear regression model with two predictor variables $z_1$ and $z_2$! 

Because no parameter is multiplied by another parameter ($\beta_1 \beta_2$), raised to a power ($\beta_1^2$), or trapped inside a nonlinear function ($e^{\beta_1 x}$ or $\sin(\beta_1 x)$), the loss surface with respect to $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ remains a **strictly convex quadratic bowl**. Consequently, the global minimum can be computed in a single step using the analytical OLS normal equations.

| Property | Simple Linear Regression | Polynomial Regression | Non-Parametric (e.g., Splines / Kernels) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Hypothesis Class** | Straight lines ($d=1$) | Global polynomials of degree $d$ | Localized piecewise polynomials |
| **Linear in Parameters?** | **Yes** | **Yes** | Varies by algorithm |
| **Optimization Method** | Closed-form OLS | Closed-form OLS | Quadratic programming / kernels |
| **Feature Space Mapping** | $\mathbb{R}^1 \to \mathbb{R}^1$ | $\mathbb{R}^1 \to \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ (powers) | Basis expansions / infinite Hilbert space |
| **Extrapolation Risk** | Stable (constant slope) | **Catastrophic (Runge oscillations)** | Controlled boundary constraints |
| **Complexity Dial** | None (fixed degree 1) | Degree $d$ and regularization $\lambda$ | Knot placement / bandwidth $\gamma$ |

### 4. Mathematical Formulation & The Vandermonde Matrix

Given a dataset of $n$ training pairs $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, a univariate polynomial regression model of degree $d$ expresses each observation as:

$$
y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \beta_2 x_i^2 + \dots + \beta_d x_i^d + \varepsilon_i, \quad i = 1, \dots, n
$$

We assemble all $n$ equations into compact matrix-vector notation:

$$
\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
$$

Let us inspect the dimensions of every component:

$$
\underbrace{\mathbf{y}}_{n \times 1} = \underbrace{\mathbf{X}}_{n \times (d+1)} \times \underbrace{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{(d+1) \times 1} + \underbrace{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{n \times 1}
$$

where the design matrix $\mathbf{X}$ is the celebrated **Vandermonde Design Matrix**:

$$
\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix}
  1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & \dots & x_1^d \\
  1 & x_2 & x_2^2 & \dots & x_2^d \\
  \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
  1 & x_n & x_n^2 & \dots & x_n^d
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (d+1)}
$$

Each row represents an individual sample $i$ whose raw scalar value $x_i$ has been mapped into a $(d+1)$-dimensional basis vector:

$$
\phi(x_i) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_i & x_i^2 & \dots & x_i^d \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times (d+1)}
$$

### 5. First-Principles Derivation of the OLS Normal Equations

We seek the parameter vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ that minimizes the Residual Sum of Squares (RSS):

$$
\text{RSS}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2
$$

Expressing the $L_2$ norm as a vector inner product and expanding:

$$
\begin{aligned}
J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) &= (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \\
&= \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} - \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} \\
&\quad + \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}
\end{aligned}
$$

Because the term $\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is a $1 \times 1$ scalar, it equals its own transpose $\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$. Combining cross-terms:

$$
J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y} - 2\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$

Differentiating with respect to the vector $\boldsymbol{\beta}$:

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} J = \frac{\partial J}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} = -2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + 2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$

Setting the gradient to zero vector $\mathbf{0}$:

$$
-2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + 2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}
$$

This system is the **Polynomial Normal Equations**. As long as the dataset contains at least $d+1$ distinct $x_i$ coordinates, the columns of the Vandermonde matrix are linearly independent. The square Gram matrix $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{(d+1) \times (d+1)}$ is strictly positive-definite and invertible:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}
$$

#### Geometric Projection & Residual Orthogonality
The fitted vector $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}$ is the orthogonal projection of target vector $\mathbf{y}$ onto the polynomial column subspace $\text{col}(\mathbf{X})$. 

Consequently, the residual vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is strictly perpendicular to every single polynomial power column:

$$
\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0} \implies \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^j e_i = 0, \quad \forall j \in \{0, 1, \dots, d\}
$$

For $j=0$, this guarantees that the sum of residuals is identically zero ($\sum e_i = 0$) whenever an intercept is included.

### 6. Model Fitting: Complete Worked Numerical Example

To make the matrix mechanics concrete, let us calculate a complete polynomial regression problem from scratch without rounding.

#### The Problem Dataset
Suppose an educational researcher examines study time ($x$ in hours) versus examination score ($y$ out of 15) for $n=4$ students:

| Student ($i$) | Study Hours ($x_i$) | Exam Score ($y_i$) | Polynomial Basis $[1, x_i, x_i^2]$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 1 | 0 | 2 | $[1, 0, 0]$ |
| 2 | 1 | 5 | $[1, 1, 1]$ |
| 3 | 2 | 6 | $[1, 2, 4]$ |
| 4 | 3 | 12 | $[1, 3, 9]$ |

We wish to fit a degree-2 quadratic polynomial: $\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2$.

#### Step 1: Form the Vandermonde Design Matrix
$$
\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix}
  1 & 0 & 0 \\
  1 & 1 & 1 \\
  1 & 2 & 4 \\
  1 & 3 & 9
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 3}, \qquad
\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix}
  2 \\ 5 \\ 6 \\ 12
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 1}
$$

#### Step 2: Compute the Gram Matrix $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$
Multiplying $\mathbf{X}^T$ ($3 \times 4$) by $\mathbf{X}$ ($4 \times 3$):

$$
\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix}
  1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
  0 & 1 & 2 & 3 \\
  0 & 1 & 4 & 9
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
  1 & 0 & 0 \\
  1 & 1 & 1 \\
  1 & 2 & 4 \\
  1 & 3 & 9
\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
  4 & 6 & 14 \\
  6 & 14 & 36 \\
  14 & 36 & 98
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Let's verify the diagonal entries:
- $[\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}]_{11} = 1+1+1+1 = 4$
- $[\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}]_{22} = 0^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 = 0 + 1 + 4 + 9 = 14$
- $[\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}]_{33} = 0^2 + 1^2 + 4^2 + 9^2 = 0 + 1 + 16 + 81 = 98$

#### Step 3: Compute the Moment Vector $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$
$$
\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix}
  1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
  0 & 1 & 2 & 3 \\
  0 & 1 & 4 & 9
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
  2 \\ 5 \\ 6 \\ 12
\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
  2 + 5 + 6 + 12 \\
  0(2) + 1(5) + 2(6) + 3(12) \\
  0(2) + 1(5) + 4(6) + 9(12)
\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
  25 \\
  53 \\
  137
\end{bmatrix}
$$

#### Step 4: Invert $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$
First, compute the determinant of the $3 \times 3$ Gram matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}) &= 4(14 \cdot 98 - 36^2) - 6(6 \cdot 98 - 36 \cdot 14) + 14(6 \cdot 36 - 14 \cdot 14) \\
&= 4(1372 - 1296) - 6(588 - 504) + 14(216 - 196) \\
&= 4(76) - 6(84) + 14(20) \\
&= 304 - 504 + 280 = 80
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $\det = 80 \ne 0$, the inverse exists. Computing adjugate cofactors and dividing by $80$:

$$
(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1} = \frac{1}{80} \begin{bmatrix}
  76 & -84 & 20 \\
  -84 & 196 & -60 \\
  20 & -60 & 20
\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
  \frac{19}{20} & -\frac{21}{20} & \frac{1}{4} \\[4pt]
  -\frac{21}{20} & \frac{49}{20} & -\frac{3}{4} \\[4pt]
  \frac{1}{4} & -\frac{3}{4} & \frac{1}{4}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

#### Step 5: Solve for Optimal Coefficients $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$
$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix}
  \frac{19}{20} & -\frac{21}{20} & \frac{1}{4} \\[4pt]
  -\frac{21}{20} & \frac{49}{20} & -\frac{3}{4} \\[4pt]
  \frac{1}{4} & -\frac{3}{4} & \frac{1}{4}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
  25 \\ 53 \\ 137
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Multiplying row by column:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\beta}_0 &= \frac{19(25) - 21(53) + 5(137)}{20} = \frac{475 - 1113 + 685}{20} = \frac{47}{20} = \mathbf{2.35} \\
\hat{\beta}_1 &= \frac{-21(25) + 49(53) - 15(137)}{20} = \frac{-525 + 2597 - 2055}{20} = \frac{17}{20} = \mathbf{0.85} \\
\hat{\beta}_2 &= \frac{1(25) - 3(53) + 1(137)}{4} = \frac{25 - 159 + 137}{4} = \frac{3}{4} = \mathbf{0.75}
\end{aligned}
$$

The exact OLS fitted quadratic regression equation is:

$$
\hat{y} = 2.35 + 0.85x + 0.75x^2
$$

#### Step 6: Prediction, Residual Analysis, and RSS Verification
Let us verify the predictions and sample residuals:

| $x_i$ | Observed $y_i$ | Fitted $\hat{y}_i = 2.35 + 0.85x_i + 0.75x_i^2$ | Residual $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$ | Squared Residual $e_i^2$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 0 | 2 | $2.35 + 0 + 0 = 2.35$ | $2 - 2.35 = \mathbf{-0.35}$ | $0.1225$ |
| 1 | 5 | $2.35 + 0.85(1) + 0.75(1) = 3.95$ | $5 - 3.95 = \mathbf{+1.05}$ | $1.1025$ |
| 2 | 6 | $2.35 + 0.85(2) + 0.75(4) = 7.05$ | $6 - 7.05 = \mathbf{-1.05}$ | $1.1025$ |
| 3 | 12 | $2.35 + 0.85(3) + 0.75(9) = 11.65$ | $12 - 11.65 = \mathbf{+0.35}$ | $0.1225$ |
| **Sum** | | | $\sum e_i = \mathbf{0.00}$ | $\text{RSS} = \mathbf{2.4500}$ |

![Fitted quadratic regression model showing observed points, predictions, and residuals](/images/polynomial-regression/05_worked_example_fit.png)

Both fundamental OLS properties hold to infinite precision:
1. **Sum of residuals is exactly zero:** $-0.35 + 1.05 - 1.05 + 0.35 = 0.00$.
2. **Residuals are orthogonal to design columns:**
   - $\sum x_i e_i = 0(-0.35) + 1(1.05) + 2(-1.05) + 3(0.35) = 1.05 - 2.10 + 1.05 = 0.00$.
   - $\sum x_i^2 e_i = 0(-0.35) + 1(1.05) + 4(-1.05) + 9(0.35) = 1.05 - 4.20 + 3.15 = 0.00$.

### 8. Multivariate Polynomials & Interaction Terms

In practical applications, targets depend on multiple features (e.g. predicting house price $y$ from living area $X_1$ and number of bedrooms $X_2$).

A standard multiple linear model assumes purely additive effects:

$$
\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2
$$

Expanding to degree 2 without interactions captures curvature along individual axes:

$$
\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \beta_3 X_1^2 + \beta_4 X_2^2
$$

However, pure power terms assume that the effect of living area $X_1$ is completely independent of the number of bedrooms $X_2$. In reality, adding 500 square feet to a 1-bedroom apartment has a vastly different market impact than adding 500 square feet to a 5-bedroom house.

To model this synergy, we include the **interaction term** $X_1 X_2$:

$$
\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \beta_3 X_1^2 + \beta_4 X_2^2 + \beta_5 X_1 X_2
$$

<Callout kind="tip">
  **Partial Derivative Interpretation**:
  Taking the partial derivative with respect to $X_1$:
  $$
  \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial X_1} = \beta_1 + 2\beta_3 X_1 + \beta_5 X_2
  $$
  The marginal rate of change of $\hat{y}$ with respect to $X_1$ now explicitly depends on the current value of $X_2$! When $\beta_5 \ne 0$, the features interact synergistically.
</Callout>

### 9. The Combinatorial Explosion of Feature Space

How many features are created when applying a degree-$d$ polynomial expansion to $p$ input variables?

Including the constant intercept and all cross-product interaction terms $\prod_{k=1}^p X_k^{d_k}$ with $\sum d_k \le d$, the total number of features $D$ is given by the combination with repetition formula:

$$
D = \binom{p + d}{d} = \frac{(p + d)!}{p! \, d!}
$$

| Original Features ($p$) | Degree $d=1$ | Degree $d=2$ | Degree $d=3$ | Degree $d=4$ | Degree $d=5$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **1** | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| **2** | 3 | 6 | 10 | 15 | 21 |
| **5** | 6 | 21 | 56 | 126 | 252 |
| **10** | 11 | 66 | 286 | 1,001 | 3,003 |
| **20** | 21 | 231 | 1,771 | 10,626 | 53,130 |

<Callout kind="warning">
  **The Curse of Dimensionality**: With only $p=10$ original features and degree $d=5$, the model expands into **3,003 columns**! If your training sample size $n < 3{,}003$, the matrix $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is singular and non-invertible, making unregularized OLS mathematically impossible.
</Callout>

### 10. Bias-Variance Decomposition & Runge's Phenomenon

The polynomial degree $d$ acts as the direct complexity dial of the model:

- **Underfitting (High Bias, Low Variance, e.g. $d=1$):** The model is too rigid to capture the true underlying nonlinear curvature.
- **Optimal Fit (Balanced, e.g. $d=3$):** The curve captures the true underlying pattern while smoothing over random noise.
- **Overfitting (Low Bias, High Variance, e.g. $d=11$):** The curve interpolates every training point ($\text{RSS} \to 0$), but oscillates violently between points.

![Comparison of Underfitting d=1, Optimal Fit d=4, and Overfitting d=11](/images/polynomial-regression/03_underfitting_optimal_overfitting.png)

#### Runge's Phenomenon: The Extrapolation Hazard
When fitting high-degree polynomials on equally spaced points, the curve develops violent, high-frequency oscillations near the edges of the interval, known as **Runge's Phenomenon**. 

Never use high-degree polynomials to extrapolate beyond the observed minimum and maximum training range!

![Bias-Variance Trade-off curve showing training vs test error across polynomial degrees](/images/polynomial-regression/04_bias_variance_tradeoff.png)

Mathematically, the expected prediction error at any query point $x_0$ decomposes into three orthogonal components:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[ (y - \hat{f}(x_0))^2 \right] = \underbrace{\left( f(x_0) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x_0)] \right)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[ (\hat{f}(x_0) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x_0)])^2 \right]}_{\text{Variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible Noise}}
$$

As degree $d$ rises:
- $\text{Bias}^2$ decreases monotonically because higher-degree polynomials subsume lower-degree ones.
- $\text{Variance}$ explodes because the coefficients become hypersensitive to minor shifts in the sample data.

### 11. Taming Polynomials: Regularization & Mandatory Scaling

Instead of guessing a single magic degree $d$, a powerful modern strategy is to use a flexible degree (e.g., $d=8$) combined with **regularization** to constrain coefficient magnitudes.

![Comparison of Unregularized OLS vs Ridge vs Lasso on high-degree polynomials](/images/polynomial-regression/06_regularization_comparison.png)

#### Ridge Regression ($L_2$ Penalty)
$$
J_{\text{Ridge}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^d \beta_j^2
$$
The unpenalized intercept $\beta_0$ is preserved. In matrix form:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{Ridge}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{P})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}, \quad \mathbf{P} = \text{diag}(0, 1, 1, \dots, 1)
$$

Adding $\lambda$ to the diagonal prevents singularity and dampens wild oscillations.

#### Lasso Regression ($L_1$ Penalty)
$$
J_{\text{Lasso}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^d |\beta_j|
$$
The diamond geometry of the $L_1$ penalty drives non-essential higher-order polynomial coefficients to **exact zeros**, performing automatic degree selection and interaction pruning!

#### Why Feature Scaling is Mandatory
If $x \in [1, 100]$, then:
- $x^1 \in [1, 100]$
- $x^2 \in [1, 10{,}000]$
- $x^4 \in [1, 10^8]$

On unscaled features, the condition number $\kappa(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})$ explodes past $10^{15}$, causing catastrophic arithmetic roundoff errors. Furthermore, regularizers like $\lambda \sum \beta_j^2$ would unfairly penalize higher powers simply because their unscaled values are massive. 

Always standardize features with **$z$-score normalization** after polynomial expansion:
$$
z_j = \frac{x^j - \mu_j}{\sigma_j}
$$
