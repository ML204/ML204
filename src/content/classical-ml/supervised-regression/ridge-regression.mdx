### 1. The Problem: Why Do We Need Ridge Regression?

In standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), our only goal is to minimize training error:

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2
$$

When features are uncorrelated and the sample size $n$ is vastly larger than the number of predictors $p$, OLS is optimal. However, fitting the observed training data with zero bias is **not** the true goal of machine learning. Our ultimate objective is **generalization**—making accurate predictions on brand new, unseen test data.

When predictors are strongly correlated with one another (**multicollinearity**), OLS breaks down disastrously.

![What correlation between predictors means: two features carrying overlapping information](/images/ridge-regression/01_multicollinearity.png)

#### What Is Multicollinearity?
Suppose we want to predict a person's blood pressure using two features:
1. Weight in kilograms ($x_1$).
2. Weight in pounds ($x_2$).

Because $x_2 \approx 2.2 \times x_1$, these two predictors carry virtually identical information. In matrix terms, the columns of the design matrix $\mathbf{X}$ are nearly linearly dependent.

When you attempt to solve the normal equations:
$$
\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}
$$

The matrix $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ becomes **ill-conditioned** (its determinant is near zero, and its smallest eigenvalue approaches zero).

The variance of the OLS coefficient vector is given by:
$$
\text{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{OLS}}) = \sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}
$$

When $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is nearly singular, the entries of its inverse $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ explode to enormous magnitudes!

![Simulation showing severe instability in OLS coefficient estimates across random samples](/images/ridge-regression/02_coefficient_stability.png)

As shown in the simulation above, running OLS on slightly different random samples of the same population produces wild oscillations: one sample might estimate $\hat{\beta}_1 = +520$ and $\hat{\beta}_2 = -510$, while the next yields $\hat{\beta}_1 = -340$ and $\hat{\beta}_2 = +355$. The model overfits sample noise, resulting in poor out-of-sample predictive performance.

---

### 2. Ridge Intuition: Shrinkage and Regularization

How do we tame this extreme sensitivity? We introduce **regularization**: we restrict how large the coefficients are allowed to grow.

Instead of letting OLS assign massive canceling coefficients ($+500$ and $-500$) to collinear features, **Ridge Regression** imposes a penalty on the total squared magnitude of the coefficient vector.

#### What Is Shrinkage?
Shrinkage pulls estimated coefficients toward zero. By restraining coefficient sizes:
- The model becomes far less sensitive to tiny fluctuations or measurement noise in the training sample.
- Coefficients become stable and interpretable.
- The model trades a slight increase in training error (**bias**) for a massive reduction in parameter sensitivity (**variance**).

<Callout kind="warning">
  **What Ridge Is Not**: Ridge Regression shrinks coefficients continuously toward zero, but it **never sets coefficients exactly to zero**. Ridge keeps all $p$ features in the model. If you require sparse feature selection (eliminating variables completely), you need **Lasso ($L_1$) Regularization**.
</Callout>

---

### 3. Mathematical Foundation: The Ridge Cost Function

Ridge Regression augments the classical OLS Residual Sum of Squares (RSS) with an **$L_2$ penalty** (proportional to the squared Euclidean norm of the coefficient vector):

$$
J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \underbrace{\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2}_{\text{RSS (Data Fit)}} + \underbrace{\lambda \|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2}_{L_2\text{ Penalty (Complexity)}}
$$

In explicit summation notation across $n$ samples and $p$ features:

$$
J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( y_i - \sum_{j=1}^{p} X_{ij}\beta_j \right)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2
$$

#### Deconstructing the Objective Function
- **$\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2$ (Data Fit)**: Measures how poorly the model predicts the observed training targets.
- **$\sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2$ (Model Complexity)**: Measures the overall size of the coefficient vector. Large slopes receive a heavy quadratic penalty.
- **$\lambda \ge 0$ (Tuning Hyperparameter)**: Controls the balance between fitting the data and keeping coefficients small.

| Penalty Level | Value of $\lambda$ | Optimization Behavior |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Zero Penalty** | $\lambda = 0$ | Penalty vanishes; objective is identical to unregularized **OLS**. |
| **Small Penalty** | Small $\lambda > 0$ | Mild shrinkage; stabilizes ill-conditioned matrix without hurting fit. |
| **Optimal Penalty** | Moderate $\lambda$ | Minimizes total test-set prediction error (lowest MSE). |
| **Infinite Penalty** | $\lambda \to \infty$ | Penalty dominates data fit; all coefficients shrink to zero ($\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \to \mathbf{0}$). |

#### Why Is It Called an $L_2$ Penalty?
The Euclidean length of a vector is its $L_2$ norm: $\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum \beta_j^2}$. Ridge penalizes the square of this norm: $\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2$. Squaring ensures the penalty is smooth, continuously differentiable, and convex, yielding an exact analytical closed-form solution.

---

### 4. Step-by-Step Derivation of the Ridge Estimator

Let us derive the exact matrix formula for $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{Ridge}}$ step by step. (We assume here that features are standardized and the intercept is handled separately).

#### Step 1: Write the Matrix Objective
$$
J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) + \lambda \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$

#### Step 2: Expand the Residual Product
Using the matrix transpose identity $(\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B})^T = \mathbf{A}^T - \mathbf{B}^T$:
$$
J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = (\mathbf{y}^T - \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T)(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) + \lambda \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$
Distributing terms:
$$
J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} - \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \lambda \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$
Because the scalar quantity $\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}$ equals its transpose $\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$:
$$
J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y} - 2\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \lambda \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$

#### Step 3: Combine Quadratic Terms
Using the $p \times p$ identity matrix $\mathbf{I}_p$, we write $\lambda \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\boldsymbol{\beta} = \boldsymbol{\beta}^T(\lambda \mathbf{I}_p)\boldsymbol{\beta}$. Factoring out $\boldsymbol{\beta}$:
$$
J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y} - 2\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol{\beta}^T (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p) \boldsymbol{\beta}
$$

#### Step 4: Differentiate with Respect to $\boldsymbol{\beta}$
Applying standard matrix differentiation rules:
$$
\frac{\partial (-2\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y})}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} = -2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}
$$
Because $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p$ is symmetric:
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} \left[ \boldsymbol{\beta}^T (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p) \boldsymbol{\beta} \right] = 2(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p)\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$
Combining gives the gradient:
$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} = -2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + 2(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p)\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$

#### Step 5: Set Gradient to Zero & Solve
At the global minimum:
$$
-2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + 2(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p)\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{0}
$$
$$
(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p)\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}
$$

Multiplying both sides by the inverse $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p)^{-1}$:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{Ridge}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}
$$

This is the celebrated **Closed-Form Ridge Regression Estimator**!

---

### 5. Why Does Adding $\lambda \mathbf{I}_p$ Guarantee Invertibility?

Notice the profound difference between OLS and Ridge:
- **OLS**: $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$ requires $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ to be non-singular (fails when $p > n$ or multicollinear).
- **Ridge**: $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$ is **always invertible** for any $\lambda > 0$!

#### Proof of Strict Positive Definiteness
For any non-zero vector $\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{v}^T (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p) \mathbf{v} &= \mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} \mathbf{v} + \lambda \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v} \\
&= (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v})^T(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}) + \lambda \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 \\
&= \|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}\|^2 + \lambda \|\mathbf{v}\|^2
\end{aligned}
$$
Even if $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ (collinear features), the second term $\lambda \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 > 0$ strictly exceeds zero whenever $\lambda > 0$.

In terms of eigenvalues: if the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ are $d_1, \dots, d_p \ge 0$, then the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p$ are:
$$
d_1 + \lambda, \quad d_2 + \lambda, \quad \dots, \quad d_p + \lambda
$$
Every single eigenvalue is shifted strictly away from zero by $\lambda$, guaranteeing that the matrix is non-singular, well-conditioned, and invertible.

#### A Tiny Numerical Demonstration
Consider an extreme case with two identical, perfectly collinear predictors:
$$
\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \\ 3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}
$$
Computing $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$:
$$
\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 14 & 14 \\ 14 & 14 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}) = 14(14) - 14(14) = 0
$$
OLS is completely paralyzed; it cannot invert $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$.

Now apply Ridge with $\lambda = 1$:
$$
\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 15 & 14 \\ 14 & 15 \end{bmatrix}
$$
$$
\det = 15^2 - 14^2 = 225 - 196 = 29 > 0
$$
The system inverts effortlessly! By symmetry, Ridge divides the influence equally: $\hat{\beta}_1 = \hat{\beta}_2 = \frac{28}{30} \approx 0.933$.

---

### 6. The Regularization Parameter $\lambda$ & Coefficient Paths

As we vary $\lambda$ continuously from $0$ to large values, we trace the **coefficient shrinkage paths**:

![Paths traced by regression coefficients as the regularization penalty increases](/images/ridge-regression/03_ridge_coefficient_paths.png)

1. At $\lambda = 0$: Coefficients start at their unconstrained OLS values (often wildly dispersed).
2. As $\lambda$ increases: All coefficients shrink toward zero at rates governed by the eigenvalues of the feature correlation matrix.
3. As $\lambda \to \infty$: All coefficients asymptotically approach zero, but never become strictly zero at any finite $\lambda$.

---

### 7. Geometric Intuition: The Circular $L_2$ Constraint

Ridge Regression can be equivalently formulated as a **constrained optimization problem** (Lagrangian duality):

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \text{RSS}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2 \le s
$$

where $s$ is a budget constraint inversely related to $\lambda$.

![Geometric illustration of Ridge Regression: RSS contour ellipses meeting the circular constraint boundary](/images/ridge-regression/04_ridge_geometry.png)

In 2D parameter space $(\beta_1, \beta_2)$:
- **The OLS Contours (Blue Ellipses)**: Centered at the unconstrained minimum $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{OLS}}$. Each ellipse represents parameter combinations with equal RSS.
- **The Ridge Constraint (Green Circle)**: The inequality $\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 \le s$ defines a **smooth circle (disk)** centered at the origin.
- **The Ridge Solution (Red Dot)**: The constrained optimum is the point where the expanding RSS ellipse first touches the circular boundary.

<Callout kind="tip">
  **Why Ridge Doesn't Sparsify**: Because the $L_2$ constraint boundary is a smooth circle with no sharp vertices on the coordinate axes, the elliptical contour touches the circle along its smooth arc rather than precisely on the axis. Thus, both $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ remain non-zero!
</Callout>

---

### 8. The Bias-Variance Trade-Off

Why would we deliberately add bias to our estimates? The answer lies in the **Mean Squared Error (MSE)** decomposition of an estimator:

$$
\text{MSE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2\right] = \text{Bias}^2(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) + \text{Variance}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
$$

![The Bias-Variance Trade-off: total error curve reaches a minimum at optimal lambda](/images/ridge-regression/05_bias_variance.png)

- **At $\lambda = 0$ (OLS)**: $\text{Bias} = 0$, but when predictors are correlated, $\text{Variance}$ is enormous. Total error is high.
- **As $\lambda$ increases**: We introduce a small amount of bias (coefficients are shrunk toward zero). In exchange, parameter variance drops precipitously.
- **At $\lambda^*$ (Sweet Spot)**: The reduction in variance far outstrips the minor gain in bias, yielding the lowest total prediction error!
- **As $\lambda \to \infty$**: Bias becomes dominant (underfitting), causing total MSE to rise again.

---

### 9. Feature Standardization: Why Must We Scale Predictors?

The Ridge penalty adds $\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2$. Notice that the penalty treats all coefficients symmetrically!

Suppose feature $x_1$ is household income in dollars ($\$80,000$) and feature $x_2$ is number of bedrooms ($3$).
- A one-unit change in bedrooms corresponds to a large slope (e.g., $\beta_2 = 30,000$).
- A one-unit change in dollar income corresponds to a tiny slope (e.g., $\beta_1 = 0.5$).

If features are unscaled, the penalty $\beta_2^2 = (30,000)^2 = 900,000,000$ will dwarf $\beta_1^2 = 0.25$! Ridge will brutally penalize the bedroom coefficient simply because of the units used to measure it.

![Standardization brings all features to mean zero and variance one](/images/ridge-regression/06_standardization.png)

#### Standardization Formula ($Z$-Score)
Before applying Ridge Regression, every predictor must be scaled to mean $0$ and standard deviation $1$:

$$
z_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j}{s_j}
$$

#### Never Penalize the Intercept!
The intercept $\beta_0$ represents the baseline target value when all features are zero. Penalizing $\beta_0$ would unfairly force predictions toward zero simply because of the vertical origin of $y$. In practice:
1. We center the target: $y_i \leftarrow y_i - \bar{y}$.
2. Or we omit $\beta_0$ from the penalty matrix: $\text{diag}(0, \lambda, \lambda, \dots, \lambda)$.

---

### 10. Choosing Optimal $\lambda$ via Cross-Validation

How do we pick the best value for $\lambda$? Because $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter, it cannot be learned by minimizing training error (which would always choose $\lambda = 0$).

Instead, we use **$K$-Fold Cross-Validation**:

![K-Fold Cross-Validation workflow: splitting into folds and testing on validation holdouts](/images/ridge-regression/07_cross_validation_workflow.png)

1. Split training data into $K$ equal subsets (folds).
2. For a grid of candidate $\lambda$ values: train on $K-1$ folds and evaluate validation MSE on the remaining fold.
3. Average validation errors across all $K$ iterations.
4. Select the $\lambda^*$ that achieves the minimum average cross-validation MSE.

### Summary Reference Table

| Concept | Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) | Ridge Regression ($L_2$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Objective Function** | $\min \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2$ | $\min \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2$ |
| **Closed-Form Solution** | $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$ | $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}_p)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$ |
| **Matrix Invertibility** | Requires full column rank ($\text{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p$) | **Always invertible** for any $\lambda > 0$ |
| **Multicollinearity Impact** | Wild coefficient instability and massive variance | Stabilizes estimates; shrinks coefficients smoothly |
| **Constraint Shape** | None (unconstrained) | Smooth circle/sphere ($\sum \beta_j^2 \le s$) |
| **Feature Selection** | None (all features retained) | None (coefficients shrink near zero, but never exact zero) |
| **Feature Scaling** | Scale-invariant (coefficients adjust units) | **Mandatory** (unscaled units cause unequal penalties) |
| **Intercept Treatment** | Intercept included in parameters | Intercept $\beta_0$ is **never penalized** |
